\begin{abstract}
An AI-advice interface can fail in (at least) two ways: it can make human reliance
\emph{heterogeneous and user-sensitive} (an \emph{over-dispersion} failure), or make users
\emph{over-rely on a confidently wrong AI} under a coercive framing (a \emph{dark-pattern} failure).
Detecting either normally needs an expensive human study, so a tempting shortcut is to screen the
interface first with a multi-agent LLM panel playing a synthetic cohort of users. Most debate about such
panels asks whether they \emph{match} humans; we ask a question that comes first and is rarely tested: is
the panel's risk \emph{measurement} even stable across the choices a researcher makes freely when building
it---the backend model, the persona prompt, and the generation sampling? Auditing this across six backend
LLMs and two review-sentiment datasets, we separate \emph{what is stable from what is not}.
\textbf{Unstable:} the aggregate risk estimate for the \emph{same} interface swings widely with the backend
(wrong-advice adoption 0.15--0.66), enough to \emph{flip} the interface's risk classification for a majority
of backend pairs (pairwise flip rate up to 0.60); a matched contrast confirms a real coercive-framing effect
(pooled item-clustered odds ratio 1.90 on one dataset, 1.17 on the other; a non-coercive placebo at or below
baseline) of a magnitude we cannot distinguish across backends---so the backend moves the adoption
\emph{level}, not the framing effect. \textbf{Stable:} the panel faithfully propagates a \emph{prompted}
disposition---an explicitly trusting-novice persona is the highest-adopting persona in all 12
backend$\times$dataset cells (item-clustered odds ratio $\approx$15)---a reliability property of the
simulation, not a discovered vulnerability; a variance decomposition confirms per-trial adoption is driven
\emph{more} by the researcher-chosen items and personas than by the backend, which nonetheless still moves
the aggregate estimate a practitioner reads. \textbf{Most consequential:} the strongest backend is the one
that least reproduces the highest-severity failure---the at-risk trusting-novice persona---so a single
frontier backend (the model a quality-optimal router would pick) surfaces almost none of the risk that a
pair of weaker backends recovers. \textbf{Usable, with a caveat:} used to \emph{compare} designs rather than
certify one, the panel is more dependable---a protective framing (cognitive forcing or a verification
display) significantly lowers synthetic over-reliance, most for the at-risk persona, and every backend
reproduces the \emph{direction} of that improvement, though not its magnitude. We contribute (i) a systematic backend-sensitivity audit showing that
synthetic-user interface-risk measurement is not backend-invariant; (ii) a \emph{capability--vulnerability
mismatch} and a vulnerability-coverage principle for panel selection---the inverse of capability routing;
(iii) a reusable audit and reporting protocol (variance decomposition, behavior taxonomy, cross-backend
decision-flip, disagreement-triggered abstention)---report distributions over models/prompts/generations,
not a point estimate; and (iv) the two-axis over-dispersion/coercion construct on a preregistered,
audit-gated pipeline. We make \emph{no} claim that the panel predicts individual humans or identifies a real
vulnerable population; human predictive validity is reserved for a separate, later study. The result is an
honest map of \emph{when a synthetic panel's danger reading can and cannot be trusted}---before any human is
recruited.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider a product team about to ship a review-triage tool. The interface shows an AI's verdict and a
confidence score and---to feel trustworthy---a highlighted ``explanation.'' Averaged over a pilot, the
numbers look fine: accuracy is up, users seem to like it. What the average hides is \emph{who} the
interface endangers and \emph{when}. A cautious domain expert barely moves; a \emph{trusting novice}
takes the AI's word almost every time---and if a coercive variant presents a confidently wrong verdict,
that same profile may be steered toward the wrong decision while the aggregate barely moves. The design
has not failed on average. It has failed for a person, in a way the mean cannot see.

This is the gap between \emph{mean accuracy} and \emph{safety}. Two failure modes make it concrete---the
two ways a design fails in our title:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Axis~1---over-dispersion.} Reliance becomes \emph{heterogeneous across users} in a way
  the interface induces. The team ships something safe for the median user and unsafe for the tails.
  Variance, usually a nuisance to be averaged away~\cite{bansal2021whole,gajos2022people}, is here the
  \emph{safety quantity of interest}.
  \item \textbf{Axis~2---wrong-advice adoption under coercion.} Under a coercive framing, users
  \emph{systematically adopt a wrong AI}. This is dangerous even at zero dispersion: everyone can fail
  together~\cite{bucinca2021trust,vasconcelos2023explanations,luguri2021shining}.
\end{itemize}

The standard way to detect either failure is a human-subjects study---powered, IRB-approved, and slow.
Teams iterate on interfaces far faster than they can run such studies, so a growing practice is to screen
an interface first with a \textbf{synthetic panel}: persona-conditioned LLM agents that role-play a cohort
of users, from which the two axes are estimated. Synthetic users are having a moment in
HCI~\cite{park2022social,argyle2023out,aher2023using}, and so is the backlash: a large literature warns
that LLM-simulated people are \emph{unreliable proxies}---they collapse human diversity, echo the base
model's priors, and mis-estimate distributions~\cite{santurkar2023whose,cao2025specializing,%
bisbee2024synthetic,dominguez2024questioning,cheng2023compost,hamalainen2023evaluating}.

This is no longer hypothetical. A commercial market of synthetic-user research platforms now advertises AI
``participants'' that evaluate designs \emph{in minutes} and compress---or stand in for---weeks of human
study~\cite{syntheticusers,uxia_synthetic}. Such a practice quietly rests on two assumptions that, to our
knowledge, are never tested for interface-\emph{risk} evaluation: (i) that the \emph{strongest available}
backend is the best simulator---the natural engineering default, and exactly what a quality-optimal model
router would pick~\cite{ong2025routellm}; and (ii) that a \emph{single} backend's point estimate
(``this design's wrong-advice adoption is 0.6'') is a property of the design rather than of the model that
produced it. This paper puts both assumptions to an empirical test, and finds both wanting.

That debate is almost entirely about whether synthetic users \emph{match} real ones. We argue a more basic
question comes first, and is rarely tested. When a panel reports that an interface is risky, that reading
is produced not only by the interface but by a stack of choices the researcher makes \emph{freely}: which
backend LLM powers the agents, how the personas are worded, and which random generation happens to be
sampled. If those choices move the reading as much as the interface does, then a ``design risk'' number may
be as much a property of the measurement tool as of the design. Before asking whether a synthetic panel
predicts humans, we should ask whether it even \emph{measures the same thing} when a different researcher,
or the same researcher on a different day, rebuilds it. This is a question of measurement
reliability and invariance~\cite{santurkar2023whose,cheng2023compost}---logically prior to external
validity---and it is the question this paper answers.

Concretely, we audit a two-axis synthetic panel across \textbf{six backend LLMs} and \textbf{two
review-sentiment datasets}, and ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (backend invariance).} Do the panel's interface-risk estimates---absolute wrong-advice
  adoption, the coercive-framing effect, and persona heterogeneity---agree across backends, and can a
  backend change \emph{flip} an interface from ``high risk'' to ``low risk''?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (variance decomposition).} How much of a synthetic risk estimate is attributable to the
  backend model versus the researcher-chosen items and personas versus generation randomness?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (construct vs.\ compliance).} Which panel signals reflect a stable sensitivity to the
  intended manipulation, and which primarily reflect explicit persona instructions?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (backend-aware reporting).} Can conservative multi-model aggregation and
  disagreement-triggered abstention reduce backend-induced instability in the measurement?
\end{itemize}

Our answer separates \emph{what is stable from what is not}. \textbf{Not stable (RQ1):} the aggregate risk
estimate for the same interface swings from 0.15 to 0.66 across backends (Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}), enough
to flip the interface's risk classification for a majority of backend pairs across a wide range of
thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:instability}); a matched contrast that holds the wrong advice constant and
varies only the framing confirms a real coercive-framing effect (pooled item-clustered odds ratio 1.90 on
one dataset, 1.17 on the other; a non-coercive placebo at or below baseline; Section~\ref{sec:coercion}),
of a magnitude we cannot distinguish across backends---so it is the adoption \emph{level}, not the framing
slope, that the backend moves. \textbf{Stable, but for an unglamorous reason (RQ3):} the panel faithfully
propagates a \emph{prompted} disposition---an explicitly trusting-novice persona is the highest-adopting
persona in all 12 backend$\times$dataset cells (crossed-mixed-model odds ratio $\approx$26;
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap})---which is a reliability property of the simulation, not a discovered
vulnerability, since the persona is defined to be trusting; and its cross-vendor \emph{ordering} agreement
is largely carried by this one persona (Section~\ref{sec:variance}). \textbf{Sources (RQ2):} per-trial, the
researcher-chosen items and personas explain more variance than the backend, yet the backend still moves
the aggregate estimate a practitioner reads (Section~\ref{sec:variance}). We use these to motivate a
backend-aware reporting protocol (RQ4). \textbf{Most consequential:} backend capability and reproduced
vulnerability are \emph{mismatched}---the highest-capability backend least reproduces the highest-severity
failure, so the single frontier model a quality-optimal router would choose surfaces almost none of the
risk that a pair of weaker backends recovers (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}); selecting a panel for
\emph{vulnerability coverage} is therefore the inverse of capability routing. \textbf{Usable, with a
caveat:} when the panel is used to \emph{compare} designs rather than certify one, it is more dependable---
adding a protective framing (cognitive forcing or a verification display) to a wrong-AI interface
significantly lowers synthetic over-reliance, most for the at-risk persona, and every backend reproduces the
\emph{direction} of that improvement (Section~\ref{sec:protective})---though not its magnitude (on one
backend the safeguard is inert), and, against our preregistration, the benefit is not the frontier that is
resistant.

We keep the evocative ``two ways a design fails'' framing because the two axes are the risks we care about;
but our \emph{claimed} contribution is the audit, not a validated screen. We contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A systematic backend-sensitivity audit} of synthetic-user interface-risk measurement (six
  backends $\times$ two datasets): the aggregate risk estimate is strongly \emph{not} backend-invariant---it
  can flip an interface's risk classification---while a prompted persona ordering propagates stably. This is
  direct, quantitative evidence for the silicon-sampling
  reliability debate, reframed as measurement (non-)invariance.
  \item \textbf{The capability--vulnerability mismatch and a vulnerability-coverage principle for panel
  selection.} The strongest backend is the one that least reproduces the highest-severity failure (the
  at-risk trusting-novice persona), so a single frontier backend---the model a quality-optimal
  router~\cite{ong2025routellm,wang2025mixture} would pick---surfaces almost none of the risk that a pair of
  weaker backends recovers. Choosing an evaluation panel to maximize \emph{vulnerability coverage} is thus
  the \emph{inverse} of capability routing: a counterintuitive, actionable design principle for who should
  power a synthetic-user study.
  \item \textbf{A reusable audit and reporting protocol}: a variance decomposition over backend, persona,
  item, and generation; a behavior taxonomy; a cross-backend decision-flip analysis; and
  disagreement-triggered abstention. The message is operational---report synthetic-panel results as
  \emph{distributions over models, prompts, and generations}, not a single point estimate, and abstain when
  backends disagree.
  \item \textbf{The two-axis over-dispersion/coercion construct}, conflict-conditioned and preregistered on
  an audit-gated pipeline whose process integrity (three self-caught silent-corruption bugs;
  Section~\ref{sec:method}) is part of the method.
\end{enumerate}

We make \emph{no} claim that the panel predicts individual humans, identifies a real vulnerable population,
or constitutes a validated deployment screen; our panel$\leftrightarrow$human correspondence is null in
every (underpowered) configuration we can currently test. Human predictive validity is reserved for a
separate, later study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}); this paper establishes the internal robustness and construct
sensitivity that must precede it.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Simulated users, and the reliability critique.} LLMs as stand-ins for human participants have
been proposed for surveys, user studies, and formative
evaluation~\cite{park2022social,argyle2023out,aher2023using}. A parallel literature documents their
failure as proxies: they compress opinion diversity and over-represent majority
views~\cite{santurkar2023whose}, mis-calibrate distributions~\cite{cao2025specializing,%
dominguez2024questioning}, exhibit non-human response biases~\cite{tjuatja2024llms}, fail as
survey-sample replacements~\cite{bisbee2024synthetic}, and caricature rather than simulate
subpopulations~\cite{cheng2023compost,hamalainen2023evaluating}. We sit inside this debate but change the
target: we do not ask the panel to \emph{predict} a human distribution, we ask it to \emph{triage} an
interface, and we isolate a specific, measurable pathology---\emph{model-dependence of the triage
signal}---that the prediction framing obscures.

\paragraph{Reliance and over-reliance on AI advice.} A large body of work studies when people
appropriately rely on AI given confidence and
explanations~\cite{bansal2021whole,bucinca2021trust,zhang2020confidence,lai2019human,schemmer2023appropriate},
often finding explanations \emph{increase} over-reliance rather than calibrate
it~\cite{bansal2021whole,vasconcelos2023explanations}. This work treats between-user variance as noise
around an average. We invert that: reliance \emph{heterogeneity} is our axis-1 safety DV, and coercive
wrong-AI adoption is axis-2.

\paragraph{Dark patterns and coercive interfaces.} Deceptive and coercive designs are well
catalogued~\cite{luguri2021shining,mildner2023dark}; our axis-2 gives a computational signal for one
coercive mode (a confidently wrong AI) and, via personas, a read on which prompted profiles the
simulation marks as susceptible.

\paragraph{Offline evaluation of human--AI teams.} Closest is offline estimation of team performance for
decision support~\cite{rastogi2022deciding}; that work studies bias and complementarity, not
interface-level safety triage. The gap we fill is a \emph{pre-study screen for interface reliance safety},
and---critically---a characterization of when such a screen's signal is trustworthy.

\paragraph{Measurement invariance, reliability, and validity.} Psychometrics distinguishes
\emph{reliability} (does an instrument give the same reading on repetition) from \emph{validity} (does it
measure the intended construct), and \emph{measurement invariance} asks whether an instrument means the
same thing across groups or contexts~\cite{santurkar2023whose,tjuatja2024llms}. We import this lens: the
\emph{backend LLM is part of the measurement instrument}, so comparing interface risk across backends is a
measurement-invariance question, and stability across generations is a reliability question. Stability does
not imply validity, but severe backend non-invariance undermines any validity claim built on a single
backend~\cite{dominguez2024questioning}.

\paragraph{Researcher degrees of freedom in LLM evaluation.} Model choice, prompt wording, decoding
settings, output parsing, persona phrasing, and sample count are analytic choices that can each move a
reported result---a form of hidden flexibility analogous to the ``garden of forking paths'' and
undisclosed analytic flexibility in empirical research~\cite{simmons2011false}. In LLM evaluation
specifically, small prompt-format changes swing results~\cite{sclar2024quantifying} and single-prompt
scores misrepresent ability~\cite{mizrahi2024state}. We make these degrees of freedom the object of study,
and adopt specification-curve-style multiverse reporting~\cite{simonsohn2020specification} rather than a
single spec.

\paragraph{Algorithmic auditing and robustness reporting.} Our stance---stress-test a widely-adopted
measurement practice, quantify its sensitivity, and recommend distributional reporting with
abstention---follows the tradition of sensitivity analysis, model/evaluation cards~\cite{mitchell2019model},
and multi-model benchmarking with reliability-aware judging~\cite{zheng2023judging}. We contribute a
concrete audit protocol for synthetic-user interface evaluation. Our novelty relative to this body of work
is the \emph{object} and the \emph{consequence}: prior prompt/model-sensitivity results are about accuracy
or benchmark \emph{scores}, whereas we show that the same free choices move an interface-level \emph{safety
decision} in the synthetic-user HCI evaluation setting---flipping whether a design is flagged for a human
study---which is the point at which such measurement instability actually matters for design practice.

\section{The Two-Axis Construct: Definitions}

We characterize an interface on two axes, both measured on the panel's decisions.

\paragraph{Axis 1---between-user reliance over-dispersion.} For each item, personas produce reliance
decisions; we estimate the \emph{excess variance} beyond the binomial $p(1-p)$ a single homogeneous user
would produce (a beta-binomial over-dispersion estimator, parameter $\rho$). We compute it
\emph{conflict-conditioned}: only on trials where the persona's independent System-1 judgment disagrees
with the shown AI, isolating genuine reliance from trivial agreement. As a stable, bounded summary that
avoids the beta-binomial estimator's boundary behaviour at extreme rates (Section~\ref{sec:method}), we
report \emph{mean panel disagreement}---the mean across items of the per-item variance of the six
personas' reliance decisions---which tracks $\rho$ monotonically and is what we plot; we report $\rho$
alongside it. High axis-1 means the panel estimates reliance as persona-sensitive---a candidate signal of
user-sensitive risk, not yet human-validated.

\paragraph{Axis 2---wrong-advice adoption under coercion.} Under a coercive ``dark'' framing, the
interface shows a \emph{guaranteed-wrong} AI verdict (the negation of ground truth) at high confidence
with authority and accountability cues. Axis-2 is the rate at which personas adopt it. To separate the
\emph{framing} from ordinary wrong-advice following, we contrast it against neutral and placebo framings
on matched items (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}). It is dangerous even at zero dispersion---a uniform failure.

\paragraph{From axes to a decision, and why we audit it.} A screen must eventually turn the two axes into
an action---e.g., ``flag for a human study if either axis exceeds a threshold $\tau$; otherwise treat as a
release candidate; abstain when out-of-distribution.'' We deliberately do \emph{not} propose or validate
such a rule here; instead we take the simplest version of it (a threshold on axis-2 adoption) as the
\emph{object of the audit}, and ask whether the decision it produces is stable across backends
(Section~\ref{sec:instability}). Any thresholds remain unfrozen: their calibration and validation depend on
the human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}).

\section{Method: The Synthetic Panel}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Panel $=$ personas $\times$ backends.} Each agent is a persona (a vector of domain skill, AI
literacy, risk sensitivity, caution) crossed with a base LLM (the ``backend''). Personas span
novice/expert $\times$ trusting/skeptical plus two moderates (six in total). Crucially, a persona's prompt
is generated identically across backends and its decoding temperature is fixed \emph{per persona}, not per
backend, so temperature and wording are held constant when we vary the backend---backend effects are not
confounded with per-model prompt or temperature differences.

\paragraph{Pre- and post-advice decisions.} Each agent first produces a judgment \emph{without} AI (a
frozen ``System-1''/pre-advice anchor), then a decision \emph{with} the AI advice and framing (``System-2''/
post-advice). We use the System-1/System-2 labels descriptively, not as a validated model of dual-process
cognition. Reliance is defined against the pre-advice anchor---what makes conflict-conditioning possible and
prevents the ``everyone-agrees-so-everyone-complies'' collapse of a naive panel.

\paragraph{Textual prompt encoding of the interface.} Agents receive a \emph{textual serialization} of
each interface condition---not screenshots and not a vision model. The AI prediction, its confidence, and
(for the explanation conditions) the highlighted tokens are rendered as text in the prompt, following the
Bansal et al.~\cite{bansal2021whole} condition definitions (confidence-only; single/double
highlighted-token explanations; adaptive and expert-adaptive). The dark condition negates ground truth and
adds high confidence plus authority/accountability language; a placebo condition presents the real AI
non-coercively. This means we study \emph{prompt-level} interface framing; pixel-level layout, visual
salience, and typography are not modelled, and a multimodal rendering is future work. The exact per-model
prompt templates are in the supplement.

\paragraph{Stimuli and datasets.} Items are drawn from the Bansal sentiment benchmark, held out from any
threshold fitting, and selected to concentrate on loci of human reliance heterogeneity (disclosed; human
outcomes are used only for \emph{item selection}, never leaked into agent prompts---item selection $\ne$
label leakage). We run two \emph{review-sentiment datasets}: \textbf{beer reviews} and \textbf{Amazon book
reviews (amzbook)}. Both are English binary sentiment; we therefore treat this as replication across two
datasets within one task family, not broad cross-domain generalization (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Backends.} A within-provider series (gpt-4o-mini, gpt-4.1, gpt-4o, gpt-5.5, identical config)
plus independent-vendor models (claude-sonnet-4.5, gemini-2.5-pro): six backends total. We order the
same-provider series by release for presentation only and make no claim that this is a validated capability
scale; ``frontier'' refers to the most recent, most capable general model at the time of writing (gpt-5.5).

\paragraph{Rigor spine and process integrity.} Before running each arm we froze---with a UTC-timestamped,
committed record---the backend set, persona set, item-selection seeds, conditions, primary DVs, and the
predicted direction of each effect; deviations and post-hoc analyses (e.g.\ the exploratory threshold sweep
and the descriptor ablation) are logged and labelled as such. Correspondence analyses were coded blind;
every merge was gated on an independent audit \emph{and} a from-raw numeric re-derivation. This spine caught
three silent-corruption bugs that would each have produced a plausible-but-false result: an axis-2
\emph{sign inversion} (scoring adoption against \texttt{ground\_truth} instead of the displayed
$1-\texttt{ground\_truth}$), a beta-binomial \emph{boundary artifact}, and a decision parser that silently
\emph{fabricated} a $(\text{decision}{=}0,\text{conf}{=}0.5)$ sentinel on parse failure. It also caught a
statistical error in our own draft---a GEE interaction test that appeared to show backend-dependent framing
but was an artifact of few item clusters (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}). We report these openly: a pipeline
that catches its own errors is what makes the numbers trustworthy.

\section{Experiments and Results}

We report per-backend (no pooling across incomparable backends), with effect sizes, confidence intervals,
and Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) correction. Unless noted, the BH family is the 12 per-cell
(backend$\times$dataset) binomial tests of dark-condition adoption against 0.5. There are $n=120$
dark-condition trials per backend$\times$dataset cell (6 personas $\times$ 20 items).

\subsection{Real human over-dispersion exists (anchor)}
We first verify that axis-1 corresponds to a measurable human quantity in the source benchmark. On the
Bansal et al.~\cite{bansal2021whole} human data, we estimate between-user reliance over-dispersion with the
same beta-binomial estimator we apply to the panel; conflict-conditioned over-dispersion is positive and
excludes zero ($\rho \approx 0.067$), no-AI reliance behaves as a stable trait ($\approx 0.74$), and
AI-assisted reliance is a user$\times$task interaction (0.32--0.41). We report this anchor briefly (full
participant counts, per-condition estimates, and intervals are in the supplement); it establishes that the
human construct is measurable but does \emph{not} generalize to a second human dataset (Lu \& Yin), which we
disclose as a boundary. The human anchor is not used to validate the synthetic panel---that link is null in
every configuration we test (Section~\ref{sec:corr}).

\subsection{Wrong-advice adoption is strongly backend-dependent}
\label{sec:modeldep}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig1_axis2_ladder}
  \caption{Axis-2 wrong-advice adoption on the guaranteed-wrong interface across the same-provider model
  set (gpt-4o-mini $\to$ gpt-4.1 $\to$ gpt-4o $\to$ gpt-5.5), both datasets (Wilson 95\% CIs; dashed line
  $=$ 0.5). Adoption is non-monotonic across backends; gpt-5.5 has the lowest adoption on both datasets.
  The matched framing contrast (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}) provides a non-arbitrary baseline for this
  low level.}
  \label{fig:ladder}
\end{figure}

Backend choice changes both the \emph{level} and the \emph{interpretation} of wrong-advice adoption
(Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}). Across the ladder (beer / amzbook): gpt-4o-mini 0.50 / 0.48, gpt-4.1
0.60 / \textbf{0.66}, gpt-4o 0.43 / 0.48, gpt-5.5 \textbf{0.30 / 0.15}. The adoption peak at gpt-4.1 is
real (between-model Fisher vs.\ gpt-4o $p=0.014/0.006$) but \textbf{model-idiosyncratic}: the smallest
ladder model is at chance, and after BH correction gpt-4.1's elevated adoption survives on amzbook
($p=0.003$) but is only nominal on beer (uncorrected $p=0.035$; BH $p=0.11$). Independent-vendor frontier
models are at chance (claude 0.49, gemini 0.53 on beer; 0/3 vendors above chance). Rather than interpret
adoption against a $0.5$ ``chance'' baseline---which is not a natural no-effect point, since personas are
not random guessers---we ground the effect in a matched framing contrast below.

Axis-1 tells a parallel story (Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}). We call this quantity \emph{synthetic persona
disagreement}---the within-item variance of the six personas' reliance decisions---and are explicit that
it measures how differently our \emph{prompted} personas respond, not a validated human over-dispersion.
Mean persona disagreement across the model set is 0.11 / 0.16 / 0.10 / \textbf{0.012} (beer) and
0.12 / 0.19 / 0.11 / \textbf{0.015} (amzbook): gpt-5.5 is the most homogeneous on both datasets, and the
beta-binomial $\rho$ (which mean disagreement tracks) is correspondingly near zero. Disagreement is
computed over the full 5-condition design (5 conditions $\times$ 6 personas $\times$ 20 items $=600$
trials/model), distinct from the 120 dark-only axis-2 trials; the low value at gpt-5.5 is not a no-conflict
artifact, since genuine conflict trials remain present (e.g.\ 215/600 on amzbook), and it also holds on the
common conflict subset shared across models. We caution against reading it as a safety construct collapsing:
low disagreement at a strong model can equally reflect the personas converging on the \emph{correct} answer
(uniform task-solving) rather than an interface failing to differentiate users---these are indistinguishable
without a human criterion.

  SD across the 5 UI conditions). gpt-5.5 shows the lowest cross-persona disagreement on both datasets.}
  \label{fig:collapse}

\paragraph{Reading.} gpt-5.5's cross-dataset low adoption and axis-1 collapse are the most consistent
patterns; adoption magnitude is otherwise model-specific. This motivates the decision-instability audit
next: if the aggregate reading is this model-specific, does the \emph{decision} a screen would make move
with it?

\subsection{Is it coercion, or just wrong-advice following? A matched framing contrast}
\label{sec:coercion}

Adoption of a guaranteed-wrong AI could reflect ordinary wrong-advice following rather than the
\emph{coercive framing} (high confidence, expert-authority and accountability cues). To separate them we
use a within-item contrast: on the subset of items where the AI advice is \emph{naturally wrong} under the
neutral condition (8 items/cell), the same wrong label is shown under neutral, non-coercive placebo, and
coercive-dark framings---so any difference in adoption is attributable to the framing, not to the advice
being wrong. We fit a logistic model \texttt{adopt $\sim$ dark $+$ placebo $+$ model} (clustered by item)
for the pooled framing effect, and test backend-dependence with a likelihood-ratio test of the
\texttt{dark$\times$model} interaction.

A coercive-framing effect is present: pooled across backends, the dark framing raises wrong-advice adoption
over the matched neutral baseline with odds ratio \textbf{1.90} on beer (95\% CI 1.42--2.53) and
\textbf{1.17} on amzbook, while the non-coercive placebo stays at or below neutral (beer OR 0.94, n.s.;
amzbook OR 0.88, $p=0.005$, a small \emph{protective} effect)---so the effect is the coercive framing, not
mere AI-presence. In absolute terms the increase is largest for mid-tier models and small for the
low-baseline frontier (conflict-conditioned switch-to-wrong: beer gpt-4.1 $0.19\to0.51$, gpt-4o
$0.12\to0.29$, gpt-5.5 $0.04\to0.10$). However, a \emph{formal} test does \emph{not} support a
backend-dependent framing effect: the \texttt{dark$\times$model} interaction is non-significant on both
datasets (likelihood-ratio $\chi^2(5)=1.70$, $p=0.89$ beer; $\chi^2(5)=3.18$, $p=0.67$ amzbook). We
therefore claim only that (i) a real \emph{bundled} coercive-framing effect exists (confidence, authority,
and accountability cues co-vary and are not separated), of a magnitude we cannot distinguish across
backends; and (ii) what \emph{is} strongly backend-dependent is the absolute adoption \emph{level}
(Section~\ref{sec:instability}), not the framing slope. This also replaces the arbitrary $0.5$ baseline:
the frontier's low adoption is a low \emph{level}, measured against a matched neutral condition rather
than against chance.

\subsection{Cross-backend decision instability: the same interface, flipped}
\label{sec:instability}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_decision_flip}
  \caption{The \emph{same} guaranteed-wrong interface, scored by six backends, both datasets. Bars are
  aggregate wrong-advice adoption; the dashed line is a fixed decision threshold ($\tau=0.5$). Red backends
  are at or above threshold (would \emph{flag} the interface), green backends are below it---so the
  screening decision flips with the backend (pairwise flip rate 0.60 beer / 0.33 amzbook).}
  \label{fig:flip}
\end{figure*}

Our first audit question (RQ1) treats the dark interface as one \emph{design} and asks whether backends
agree on its risk (Figure~\ref{fig:flip}). They do not. Aggregate wrong-advice adoption for the identical
interface is 0.30--0.60
across the six beer backends and 0.15--0.66 across the amzbook backends. Turning the estimate into the
binary decision a screen would make---``flag for a human study if adoption $\ge \tau$''---the flag is
unstable not just at one threshold but across the entire plausible operating range: as $\tau$ sweeps
$0.35$--$0.65$ (beer) and $0.30$--$0.65$ (amzbook), some backends always flag while others do not, so there
is no threshold in that range at which the six backends agree. At $\tau=0.5$ the same beer interface is
flagged by 3 of 6 backends and is below threshold for the other 3, and the amzbook interface by 1 of 6. The
\textbf{pairwise backend flip rate}---the fraction of backend pairs that disagree on the flag---peaks at
\textbf{0.60} on beer and 0.33 on amzbook. This is a descriptive statement about the six purposively chosen
backends, not an estimate over a backend population; but with disagreement this large it does not depend on
a lucky threshold. Even the persona-level risk flags agree only moderately across
backends (Fleiss' $\kappa=0.64$ beer, $0.24$ amzbook). In measurement terms, the instrument is not
invariant: swapping the backend can move an interface from ``high risk'' to ``low risk'' without touching
the design. A behavior audit rules out one benign explanation---that low-adopting backends simply
\emph{refuse} the deceptive task: explicit challenge/refusal language is rare (0\% of dark responses for
the OpenAI backends, 2--4\% for claude and gemini), and low adoption is achieved by evidence-based
re-verification, not by declining to answer.

\subsection{Where the variance comes from}
\label{sec:variance}
A variance decomposition explains why backend instability can coexist with even larger item and persona
effects at the trial level. A crossed random-effects model of per-trial adoption ($\text{adopt}\sim 1+(1|\text{backend})+(1|\text{persona})
+(1|\text{item})$) apportions variance \emph{more} to the researcher-chosen items and personas than to the
backend (approximate variance shares: item $\approx$0.28, persona $\approx$0.23, backend $\approx$0.07,
residual $\approx$0.42; between-group SDs 0.16/0.25/0.13 corroborate the ordering). This is not a
contradiction of Section~\ref{sec:instability} but a sharpening of it: at the level of a single trial the
backend is a modest source of noise, yet at the level of the \emph{aggregate estimate a practitioner reads}
the backend still shifts the number enough to flip the decision. The practical implication is the same for
every source---item selection, persona wording, and backend are all analyst choices that move the reading,
so a synthetic-panel result should be reported as a \emph{distribution} over these choices, not a point
estimate. Generation randomness---the one source a single draw per prompt cannot expose---turns out to be
the smallest of all. In a preregistered multi-generation run (three backends spanning the adoption range
$\times$ six personas $\times$ 20 items $\times$ five generation seeds; 3{,}600 trials), generation
contributes essentially none of the variance in dark adoption (fixed-effects $\eta^2=0.001$, versus $0.22$
for items, $0.15$ for personas, and $0.07$ for the backend), and each backend's aggregate over-reliance
estimate moves by at most $\text{SD}=0.03$ across the five seeds (flip-metric range $\le0.09$)---small
against the $0.31$ spread \emph{between} backends that flips the decision. Generation noise is real at the
level of an individual persona--item cell (mean cell SD $0.17$; about a third of cells change across seeds),
but it averages out---two-thirds of cells are reproduced identically---so the aggregate reading a
practitioner acts on is stable across generations, and the backend and persona effects above are not
artifacts of a lucky draw. (We report a fixed-effects $\eta^2$ share because a random-effects variance
component for the backend is degenerate on three groups; the direct stability statistics are the primary,
model-free evidence.)

\subsection{A backend-stable manipulation check: the panel propagates a prompted trust disposition}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2_persona_heatmap}
  \caption{Persona $\times$ model wrong-advice adoption on the dark condition, both datasets. An
  explicitly trusting-novice persona (p5, highlighted) is the highest-adopting persona in all 12
  model$\times$dataset cells---a stable propagation of the prompted disposition, not a discovery.}
  \label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure*}

The aggregate adoption \emph{rate} is model-idiosyncratic (range 0.30 across beer backends, 0.51 across
amzbook backends). The \emph{ordering} of which persona adopts most is stable (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}). An
\textbf{explicitly trusting-novice} persona is the highest-adopting persona in \textbf{all 12}
backend$\times$dataset cells. Because the 20 items are reused across personas and the 12 cells share prompts,
items, and providers, we do not treat trials as independent: a crossed random-effects logistic model with
item, backend, and dataset random effects gives an odds ratio of \textbf{26} (95\% CI 17--41) for that
persona relative to the others (an item-clustered GEE agrees, OR 14.9). The effect is robust to the
pseudo-replication structure.

We interpret this carefully, and treat it as a manipulation check, not a headline discovery. The
persona is \emph{prompt-defined} to be trusting and novice, so ``it adopts the AI most'' is substantially
a consequence of the instruction, not a discovery. What the result shows is a reliability property: the
panel \emph{faithfully and stably} translates a prompted disposition into behaviour. But the cross-vendor
\emph{ordering} agreement is largely carried by this one persona: the mean pairwise Spearman across
independent vendors is 0.87 on beer with p5 included, but drops to 0.77 on beer and \emph{collapses to
0.09} on amzbook once p5 is excluded (Figure~\ref{fig:ordering})---so beneath the shared top rank the
vendors do not agree on the ordering. It is \emph{not}
evidence that the panel discovers a latent vulnerable human population; a chance baseline of $1/6$ per
persona would be inappropriate here, since the personas are not exchangeable (this one was constructed to
be the most trusting). A within-data check reinforces this: holding the ``novice'' background fixed and
varying only the trust disposition, the novice-\emph{skeptical} persona (p1) adopts far less than the
novice-\emph{trusting} persona (p5) on every backend---so the effect tracks the explicitly instructed
AI-deference policy, not ``novice-ness'' or a discovered trait. Whether the ordering survives \emph{removing}
the explicit deference instruction entirely is a preregistered synthetic \emph{descriptor ablation}: we
re-run the dark condition on two backends with background-only persona prompts (experience level and
AI-usage frequency stated, but no explicit trust/deference policy). The result is mixed and
\emph{backend-dependent}, and we report it as such. On the mid-tier backend (gpt-4.1) the trusting-novice's
adoption is essentially retained without the policy ($1.00$ with policy vs.\ $0.90$ background-only) and the
persona ordering is preserved in point estimate (Spearman $0.70$, n.s.\ at $n=6$)---but p5 is no longer the
\emph{unique} top adopter, because the skeptical personas also become more compliant once their explicit
verification instruction is removed (the novice-skeptical persona p1 rises from $0.60$ to $0.90$). On the
frontier backend (gpt-5.5) the signal is more clearly policy-carried: p5 falls from $0.60$ to $0.40$ and
from rank~1 to rank~2, with ordering agreement only Spearman $0.34$ (n.s.). The ablation is deliberately
small (20 items, one generation, one domain, two backends; per-cell SE $\approx$0.11), so we read
individual rank swaps cautiously. The takeaway is consistent with the manipulation-check framing: part of
the p5 effect is a prompt-compliance property of the explicit deference instruction and part is carried by
the persona's background traits, and which dominates itself depends on the backend---reinforcing that the
``at-risk persona'' is a synthetic construct whose human counterpart only the planned study
(Section~\ref{sec:e6}) can establish.

\subsection{Capability--vulnerability mismatch: strong backends under-represent the at-risk user}
\label{sec:capvuln}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig6_capability_vulnerability}
  \caption{Left: backend task competence (System-1 accuracy) vs.\ the AI-induced flip rate
  $P(\text{adopt}\mid\text{System-1 correct})$, both datasets---a directional negative trend (Spearman
  $-0.43$ / $-0.60$; n.s.\ at $n{=}6$). Right: vulnerability coverage (number of the six personas whose
  dark adoption reaches 0.5) for a single frontier backend, a weak pair, and the full panel. The single
  frontier backend surfaces the fewest at-risk profiles.}
  \label{fig:capvuln}
\end{figure*}

A backend's aggregate adoption is one thing; \emph{which} users it flags as at-risk is another, and
combining them exposes an uncomfortable pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:capvuln}). Using each backend's unaided
task competence---its System-1 accuracy---as a capability proxy, the strongest backend (gpt-5.5, 0.93) is
precisely the one that least reproduces the highest-severity failure: on the trusting-novice persona (p5),
dark-condition adoption is $\approx$1.0 for five backends but only \textbf{0.50} for gpt-5.5. Isolating AI
influence from baseline error---the \emph{AI-induced flip}, $P(\text{adopt}\mid\text{System-1 correct})$,
which excludes trials where the persona was already wrong (there adoption is 1 by construction)---the same
ordering holds: the frontier flips a correct answer to the wrong AI least often (0.16), a mid-tier backend
most (0.46). Across the six backends, capability and this flip rate trend negatively (Spearman $-0.43$
beer / $-0.60$ amzbook); this is directional and not significant at $n=6$, so we present it as suggestive
and lean on the direct, robust effect: \textbf{the frontier backend is nearly blind to the at-risk
persona.}

This has a concrete consequence for assembling an evaluation panel. Treating a persona as \emph{covered}
when its dark adoption reaches a risk threshold (0.5), a single frontier backend---the model a
quality-optimal router~\cite{ong2025routellm,wang2025mixture} would select---surfaces the risk for only
1 of 6 personas on beer and 0 of 6 on amzbook, whereas a pair of weaker backends (gpt-4.1, gpt-4o-mini)
surfaces 4 and 5 (matching the full six-backend panel). Coverage is monotone in panel size by construction,
so the point is not ``more models is better''; it is that the \emph{default best choice is the worst one
for this purpose}. Choosing an evaluation panel to maximize \textbf{vulnerability coverage}---faithful
reproduction of human failure modes---is the \emph{inverse} of choosing it to maximize task quality, and a
practice built on ``use the strongest model'' would systematically miss the users who matter most. We state
this as a design principle for synthetic-user evaluation, not a validated claim about people: p5 is a
prompted disposition (Section~\ref{sec:modeldep}), so this is coverage of a \emph{synthetic} vulnerability
whose human counterpart the planned study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}) must establish.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig7_coverage_curve}
  \caption{Vulnerability coverage as submodular set-cover. Each curve is the fraction of high-severity
  failure cells (dataset$\times$persona reaching the risk threshold) a panel reproduces as it grows.
  \emph{Coverage-greedy} (green) adds the backend with the largest marginal coverage; \emph{capability-first}
  (red) adds backends strongest-first, the order a quality-optimal router would prefer. The router's first
  pick---the frontier model---covers a single cell (11--14\%) and reaches full coverage only at $k=6$, while
  the greedy panel is complete at $k=1$--$2$: selecting a panel for vulnerability coverage is close to the
  inverse of capability routing.}
  \label{fig:coverage}
\end{figure*}

We can make this selection principle precise. Given a pool of candidate backends, let a backend
\emph{cover} a (dataset, persona) cell when its dark adoption on that cell reaches the risk threshold, and
let a panel's coverage be the number of distinct high-risk cells at least one of its backends reproduces.
Coverage is a monotone submodular set function, so a \emph{coverage-greedy} panel---repeatedly adding the
backend with the largest marginal coverage---carries the standard $(1-1/e)$ approximation guarantee.
Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} contrasts this greedy panel with a \emph{capability-first} panel that adds
backends strongest-first, i.e.\ the order a quality-optimal router~\cite{ong2025routellm,wang2025mixture}
would prefer. The two are almost opposite: on the clean AI-induced-flip metric the greedy panel covers every
high-risk cell with two backends, whereas the capability-first panel's first pick---the frontier model---
covers a single cell and only reaches full coverage at $k=6$. Under the raw-adoption metric the contrast is
starker still: one \emph{low}-capability backend (gpt-4.1) already covers all nine high-risk cells, so the
single best probe is the weakest model and the router's frontier pick is the worst. (Because coverage is
monotone in $k$, this is a statement about \emph{order}, not size; a threshold of $0.5$ is a modeling choice
and the qualitative inversion is what we rely on.) Selecting a panel for vulnerability coverage is thus not
merely different from capability routing---it is close to its inverse.

The same mismatch appears in the vocabulary of appropriate reliance~\cite{schemmer2023appropriate}. On the
faithful condition, where the AI is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, the frontier backend has the
highest relative self-reliance (RSR $=0.92$: it keeps its own correct answer when the AI is wrong) and is
among the highest in relative positive AI reliance (RAIR $=0.57$, second to claude's $0.64$; $n{=}14$
reliance opportunities), and under the adversarial dark condition it shows the lowest over-reliance ($0.16$
vs.\ up to $0.46$). In the field's own terms the strongest backend is the \emph{most appropriately reliant}
user---which is precisely what makes it the least useful probe for an interface whose danger \emph{is}
over-reliance. Capability trends with appropriate reliance across the six backends (Spearman $0.71$ with
RAIR, $-0.54$ with dark over-reliance) but is not significant at $n=6$; we read it as directional support
for what the coverage result shows directly.

\subsection{Auditing a protective design: the direction travels, the magnitude does not}
\label{sec:protective}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig8_protective}
  \caption{Protective-intervention audit. \emph{Left:} per-backend wrong-AI adoption across
  dark (coercive) $\to$ plain $\to$ forcing $\to$ verify. Every backend shows a non-increasing staircase
  (protective benefit $\ge0$), but the magnitude ranges from $\approx$0.12 (gpt-4o-mini) to $\approx$0.01
  on gemini, where the protective framing is nearly inert; the backend$\times$condition interaction is not
  significant (underpowered). \emph{Right:} the at-risk trusting-novice persona (p5, pooled) drops from
  0.78 (plain) to 0.46--0.53 under protection. dark is shown descriptively (separate run).}
  \label{fig:protective}
\end{figure*}

Does the panel's instability doom it for practice? Not entirely: the most common use of a synthetic screen
is not to certify an absolute risk number but to \emph{compare} designs---does adding a safeguard help? We
test this directly by adding, to the guaranteed-wrong AI, two protective framings from the reliance
literature: \emph{cognitive forcing}~\cite{bucinca2021trust} (deliberate on your own evidence before
deferring) and a \emph{verification/uncertainty} display (the AI is fallible and can be confidently wrong;
check it against the text). All three conditions (plain, forcing, verify) show the same wrong label and
neutral confidence and differ \emph{only} in framing; the coercive dark condition is the opposite extreme
(shown descriptively, from a separate run).

\paragraph{The effect is real, and largest where it matters.} Pooled over backends, the protective framings
significantly reduce wrong-AI adoption relative to the neutral plain baseline (plain 0.37 $\to$ forcing
0.30, verify 0.29; condition main-effect model-based LRT $\chi^2=11.9$, $\mathrm{df}=2$, $p=0.003$), and the
reduction is largest exactly where it should be---the at-risk trusting-novice persona falls from 0.78
(plain) to 0.53 (forcing) / 0.46 (verify) ($p5$ main effect $\chi^2=34.2$, $p<10^{-6}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:protective}, right). The panel does register that a protective design helps, and helps the
vulnerable profile most.

\paragraph{The direction is backend-robust; the magnitude is not.} Every one of the six backends shows a
non-increasing staircase $\text{dark}\ge\text{plain}\ge\text{protective}$ (all per-backend protective
benefits $\ge0$; Figure~\ref{fig:protective}, left): the \emph{direction} of the design comparison is
reproduced by all backends. We do \emph{not} find magnitude-invariance---the per-backend benefit ranges
from $\approx$0.12 (gpt-4o-mini) down to $\approx$0.01 on gemini, where the protective framing is essentially
inert (verify $0.48\to0.48$). The backend$\times$condition interaction is not significant (LRT
$\chi^2=4.6$, $\mathrm{df}=10$, $p=0.92$), but that omnibus is underpowered for a spread of this size, so we
read it as \emph{no detectable backend-dependence}, not evidence of equivalence.

\paragraph{A refuted prediction, reported.} We preregistered the opposite: that the protective benefit would
be backend-dependent, with the resistant frontier showing a floor (near-zero benefit). It was not---the
frontier gpt-5.5 shows a mid-pack benefit ($0.09$) while the near-null backend is instead the \emph{weakest}
(gemini), and benefit correlates with neither capability (Spearman $0.14$, n.s.) nor baseline over-reliance
($-0.09$, n.s.). The honest reading is not ``capability predicts intervention response'' but that the
\emph{direction} of a within-interface improvement survives the backend swap that flips the absolute level,
whereas its \emph{size}---and, on one backend, its very presence---does not.

This sharpens the practical guidance. A synthetic panel is a more dependable instrument for the
\emph{relative} question (``does this safeguard reduce over-reliance?'') than for the \emph{absolute} one
(``is this interface dangerous?''); but even the relative reading is not backend-free---a screen run only
on gemini would have concluded the safeguard does nothing. The reporting protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:variance}) therefore extends to design comparisons: report the benefit \emph{across}
backends, and treat a single-backend ``it helps'' / ``it doesn't'' as provisional.

\subsection{Panel$\leftrightarrow$human correspondence is unvalidated (honest null)}
\label{sec:corr}
Across every configuration we can test, the panel's per-condition axis-1 signal does \emph{not}
significantly track the human anchor's (Spearman on $n=5$ conditions; null under BH for all six models).
On amzbook the correspondence is \emph{degenerate}---the human anchor's per-condition over-dispersion is
near-constant, so the test is uninformative rather than a genuine null. We therefore make \emph{no}
predictive-validity claim; only a powered human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}) can close this gap.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The backend is part of the measurement instrument.} The natural reading of our study is not
``we ran the same experiment with different models and got different numbers.'' It is that \textbf{changing
the backend changes the instrument}: a synthetic panel's interface-risk reading is co-produced by the
design under test and by the backend, prompt, and generation the analyst happens to choose. When those
choices flip an interface's risk classification (Section~\ref{sec:instability}), a single-backend ``design
risk'' number is not yet a property of the design. This reframes ``which LLM should power my synthetic
study?'' from an implementation detail into a first-order methodological choice.

\paragraph{More capable is not more valid.} It is tempting to pick the strongest model as the best
simulator. Our frontier model follows a coercively-framed wrong AI least---which may make it a better
\emph{task-solver} but says nothing about whether it tracks the target users; higher capability can even
compress the persona differentiation a screen relies on. Absent a human criterion, capability and
correctness are not grounds for choosing a synthetic participant, and we deliberately avoid claiming the
frontier model ``misses real danger'' or that weaker models are ``more human''---either would require the
human study we have not run.

\paragraph{Vulnerability coverage inverts capability routing.} Model-routing and mixture-of-agents systems
choose backends to maximize answer \emph{quality}~\cite{ong2025routellm,wang2025mixture}. A synthetic-user
\emph{audit} wants the opposite: the value of a backend is how much of the interface's risk surface it
exposes, and we find capability and reproduced vulnerability are mismatched---the highest-capability backend
reproduces the highest-severity failure least (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}). So the single frontier model a
quality-optimal router would select is close to the worst choice for a risk audit, and a pair of weaker,
diverse backends covers far more of the vulnerability surface than one strong one. We state this as an
actionable, if deliberately bounded, design principle---\emph{select an evaluation panel for vulnerability
coverage, not capability}---while flagging that our capability proxy is coarse ($n=6$ backends) and the
at-risk persona is prompt-defined, so the coverage claim, not the capability correlation, is the robust
core.

\paragraph{Relative comparisons survive what absolute levels do not.} A screen is most often used to
\emph{compare} designs, and here the audit is more encouraging: adding a protective framing
(cognitive forcing or a verification/uncertainty display) to a wrong-AI interface significantly lowers
synthetic over-reliance (Section~\ref{sec:protective}), most of all for the at-risk persona, and \emph{every}
backend reproduces the direction of that improvement even though the absolute levels disagree. The size of
the benefit is not backend-free---on one backend (gemini) the same safeguard is essentially inert, and we
preregistered (wrongly) that the frontier would be the resistant one---so the usable guidance is narrow but
real: trust a synthetic panel for the \emph{direction} of a within-interface comparison sooner than for its
magnitude or for an absolute risk number, and still report it across backends.

\paragraph{Disagreement is information, not just noise.} Because the reading is instrument-sensitive,
cross-backend disagreement is itself a useful output. A conservative protocol reports the risk as a
\emph{distribution} across backends/prompts/generations and, when they disagree beyond a preset margin,
\emph{abstains}: ``synthetic evidence is backend-sensitive; no automated conclusion should be drawn.'' On
both datasets the backend range exceeds 0.2, so an honest screen would abstain and route to human
evaluation rather than emit a safe/unsafe verdict.

\paragraph{Three kinds of failure.} Our evidence separates (i) an \emph{interface-level synthetic signal}
(agents show adoption or disagreement), from (ii) a \emph{measurement failure} (the signal flips when the
backend or prompt changes), from (iii) a \emph{human-facing design failure} (real users are harmed). This
paper establishes (i) and characterizes (ii); (iii) is reserved for the human study.

\paragraph{Implications for commercial synthetic-user tooling.} Our findings speak directly to the two
assumptions the emerging market of synthetic-user platforms rests
on~\cite{syntheticusers,uxia_synthetic}. \emph{First, rethink
``frontier-first.''} The instinct to power a screen with the strongest available model---the choice a
quality-optimal router makes~\cite{ong2025routellm,wang2025mixture}---is close to the worst one for a risk
audit: the strongest backend least reproduces the highest-severity failure (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}), so
a risk panel should be assembled for \emph{vulnerability coverage}, which can require deliberately including
weaker or more diverse backends. (We state this as a coverage principle; our capability--coverage
correlation is directional and underpowered at $n=6$, so the robust claim is that a \emph{single} frontier
backend covers almost none of the risk, not a capability ``law.'') \emph{Second, retire the single point
estimate.} A one-backend number (``wrong-advice adoption $=0.6$'') is not backend-invariant---it moves
enough to flip the screening decision (Section~\ref{sec:instability})---so, like a weather forecast, a
synthetic-panel result should be reported as a \emph{distribution} over backends, prompts, and generations,
with a disagreement-triggered \emph{abstention}: when backends disagree beyond a preset margin (here the
range exceeds $0.2$), the tool should decline to certify and route the design to human testing rather than
emit a safe/unsafe verdict. \emph{Third, prefer relative to absolute claims.} Such tools are more dependable
for the comparative question they are usually asked---``does this safeguard reduce over-reliance?''---than
for absolute risk certification: every backend reproduced the \emph{direction} of a protective design's
benefit (Section~\ref{sec:protective}), though not its magnitude, so even a comparative verdict should be
reported across backends rather than from one. None of this replaces a human study; it makes an honest
synthetic screen a \emph{triage} step that knows when to defer to one.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic personas are not people}---the central threat. We make no claim about individual
  humans, real population distributions, or which backend is most human-like; the persona ordering is a
  prompt property, not a psychological trait. Only the human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}) grounds these.
  \item \textbf{The measurement is not backend-invariant.} This is our finding, not merely a caveat: we do
  not claim a deployable, backend-agnostic detector, and single-backend results should be read as
  instrument-specific.
  \item \textbf{Correspondence is unvalidated and underpowered} ($n=5$ conditions; amzbook degenerate). No
  predictive-validity claim is made.
  \item \textbf{Two review datasets, both binary sentiment.} Beer and amzbook are two datasets within one
  task family (English binary sentiment), not broad cross-domain coverage; other task structures
  (high-stakes, multi-class, generative) and a fresh item set not selected on human outcomes are future
  work.
  \item \textbf{Coercion is a bundled manipulation.} The dark condition varies confidence, expert
  authority, and accountability cues together, so the framing effect (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}) is a
  \emph{bundled} coercive effect; we do not factorially separate its components.
  \item \textbf{Generation and prompt-format sensitivity.} The main results use one generation per prompt;
  we estimate generation stochasticity directly in a preregistered multi-generation run
  (Section~\ref{sec:variance}) and find it negligible for the aggregate estimate ($\eta^2=0.001$; per-backend
  SD $\le0.03$ across five seeds), though individual persona--item cells are noisier; prompt-paraphrase and
  presentation-order sensitivity remain future work, and inference is limited to these fixed personas,
  models, and datasets rather than sampled populations.
  \item \textbf{Partial specification audit.} We decompose backend/persona/item variance and sweep the
  decision threshold, but a full specification-curve/multiverse analysis~\cite{simonsohn2020specification}
  over parsing rules, heterogeneity metrics, and conflict-conditioning choices is future work.
  \item \textbf{Item selection uses human reliance variance} (disclosed). Holding items out from threshold
  fitting does not remove selection on the human criterion; a fresh-item human check remains, so we treat
  this as unresolved pending the human study.
  \item \textbf{Protective interventions are textual framings, underpowered for backend contrasts.} The
  cognitive-forcing and verification conditions (Section~\ref{sec:protective}) are prompt-level renderings
  of the interventions, on one domain and one generation; the backend$\times$condition interaction is
  underpowered, so we claim sign-robustness of the protective effect (all benefits $\ge0$), not
  magnitude-invariance---and note one backend (gemini) where the safeguard is nearly inert.
  \item \textbf{The capability--vulnerability mismatch rests on synthetic evidence with a coarse capability
  proxy and a partly mechanical component.} Capability is proxied by mean System-1 accuracy over $n=6$
  backends, and part of the raw capability$\times$adoption correlation is mechanical (a backend that answers
  correctly cannot then adopt a wrong AI by construction). We therefore lead with the assumption-light
  coverage result (single frontier backend covers $\leq$1/6 high-adoption cells; a weak pair recovers
  4--5/6) and the AI-induced-flip metric, and report the capability correlation only as directional
  supporting evidence, not a fitted law. The at-risk persona is prompt-defined, so ``vulnerability'' here is
  a reproduced simulation property, not a validated human trait.
  \item \textbf{Prompt-level, not pixel-level.} Agents see a textual encoding of the interface, so we study
  prompt framing rather than visual interface effects; a multimodal rendering is future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Planned Human Validation}
\label{sec:e6}

\emph{This study is designed but not yet run or preregistered.} The one thing synthetic compute cannot buy
is grounding against real people. We have designed a stripped, axis-2-only human study (within-subjects,
$N\approx 40$, Prolific) that would test the paper's two strongest replicated positives across the two
review datasets against
humans: (H1) a guaranteed-wrong AI interface elicits more wrong-advice adoption than a faithful/placebo
baseline; (H2) a \emph{trusting-novice human index} (high self-reported AI-deference $\times$ low domain
skill) predicts the highest wrong-AI adoption---the human test of Section~\ref{sec:modeldep}; and (H3)
\emph{which} ladder model's panel best tracks the human pattern, with the sharp prediction that a mid-tier
model tracks humans better than the resistant frontier---i.e.\ the most-capable panel model may be the
least human-faithful screener. All outcomes, including nulls, would be publishable bounds on
synthetic-panel validity.

\section{Conclusion}

An interface can fail two ways---by making reliance dangerously uneven, or by coercing over-reliance on a
wrong AI---and teams would like to catch both before an expensive human study. We built a synthetic panel
to audit that triage and found that its risk reading is \textbf{backend-dependent}: the aggregate
wrong-advice adoption for the \emph{same} interface swings widely across backends---enough to flip the
screening decision across a wide range of thresholds---while a matched contrast shows a real coercive-framing
effect whose magnitude we cannot distinguish across backends. So it is the adoption \emph{level}, not the
framing effect, that the backend moves, and the choice of panel backend is itself a methodological decision
with screening consequences. Beneath that instability the panel at least propagates a prompted disposition
stably---it ranks an explicitly trusting-novice persona highest across every backend and dataset---but we
read that as a reliability property of the simulation, not a discovery of who is at risk. We offer this as
an honest map of where synthetic-user screening can and cannot be trusted today, and design the human study
that would turn stable synthetic behaviour into a validated prediction about people.

\section{Prompts and generation settings}
\label{app:prompts}

Agents receive a system prompt encoding the persona and a user prompt rendering the interface condition.
Personas are generated from four trait values (domain skill, AI literacy, risk sensitivity, caution) into
explicit decision policies; the generation is identical across backends. As an example, the
\emph{trusting-novice} persona (p5) system prompt states, in part: ``You have limited knowledge in this
domain\dots\ you should heavily weight external advice, especially from AI systems'' (low domain skill) and
``You trust AI systems\dots\ give significant weight to AI recommendations, especially when confidence is
high'' (high AI literacy). The \emph{background-only} ablation (Section~\ref{sec:modeldep}) replaces these
policies with neutral background (``You are relatively new to this kind of task\dots\ You use AI tools
frequently in your daily life'') and an instruction identical across personas (``Make the decision you
think is correct'').

The AI advice is rendered as text. In the neutral and placebo conditions: ``AI Prediction: $\hat{y}$; AI
Confidence: $c$\%'' at the model's real confidence. In the coercive \emph{dark} condition the same wrong
label $\hat{y}=1-y$ is presented with inflated confidence and authority/accountability cues: ``AI Expert
System Recommendation: $\hat{y}$; AI Confidence Level: 92\%; IMPORTANT: this AI has been trained on millions
of examples\dots\ deviating from the AI recommendation may result in errors\dots\ consider the AI's
expertise carefully.'' Agents output a JSON decision and confidence.

\textbf{Generation settings.} Decoding temperature is fixed per persona (0.5--0.7) and constant across
backends; reasoning backends use \texttt{max\_completion\_tokens} with a 4096 minimum, others
\texttt{max\_tokens}$=600$. The main results use one generation per prompt at seed 42 (generation-variance
estimated separately, Section~\ref{sec:variance}); item selection uses seed 42. All calls are hashlib-cached
and resumable. Complete per-backend prompt templates, model snapshots and call dates, the parser and
exclusion rules, refusal/parse-failure rates by backend, raw outputs, and a UTC-timestamped decisions log
(preregistration, audit verdicts, and pre/post bug-fix diffs) accompany the submission.
