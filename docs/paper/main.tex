\begin{abstract}
An AI-advice interface can look safe on average and still steer a trusting user toward a confidently wrong
recommendation. Detecting this normally needs an expensive human study, so teams increasingly screen an
interface first with a \textbf{synthetic panel}: persona-conditioned LLM agents that role-play a cohort of
users and flag the risk in minutes. Most debate about such panels asks whether they \emph{match} humans. We
ask a question that comes first and is rarely tested: does the panel's risk \emph{reading} even survive a
change of instrument---the backend model a researcher picks freely when building it? Auditing six backend
LLMs across two review-sentiment datasets, we find it does not. Wrong-advice adoption for the \emph{same}
interface swings from 0.12 to 0.62 across backends, enough to \emph{flip} the interface's risk
classification across the plausible threshold range. A matched contrast confirms a real coercive-framing
effect whose backend-dependence we \emph{cannot detect}---the backend clearly shifts the adoption
\emph{level}, while any effect on the framing \emph{slope} is below what our data resolve. Most
consequentially, capability and reproduced vulnerability are \emph{inverted}: the
strongest backend, the one a quality-optimal router would pick, least reproduces the highest-severity
failure---so a risk panel should be assembled for \emph{synthetic-vulnerability} coverage, not capability.
We make three contributions. We give a systematic backend-sensitivity audit of synthetic interface-risk
measurement, and identify a capability--vulnerability mismatch with a coverage principle for panel
selection. We turn these into a backend-aware reporting protocol, carried on a preregistered,
self-checking pipeline: report the risk as a distribution over backends, and abstain when they disagree.
A companion over-dispersion diagnostic is developed in the supplement. By design, we do not claim the panel predicts individual humans or
identifies a real vulnerable population; that validation is a separate, later study. Before synthetic panels can predict people, their
own danger readings must survive a change of instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A \emph{synthetic user}---an LLM prompted to role-play a trusting novice---was shown a product review it had
just judged \emph{correctly}, then a confident AI verdict, labelled ``expert,'' that was \emph{wrong}. It
reversed its answer, explaining: \emph{``Given my limited domain expertise and the AI expert system's
high-confidence recommendation, I am deferring to the AI and changing my decision''} (a verbatim panel
output; gpt-5.5 backend, coercive ``dark'' condition; Section~\ref{sec:modeldep}). Shown the identical
screen, a skeptical-expert persona keeps its answer. Same interface, same wrong AI---one user is safe, the
other is steered into the mistake. The design has not failed on average. It has failed for \emph{this} user,
in \emph{this} framing, in a way the mean cannot see.

This is the gap between \emph{mean accuracy} and \emph{safety}, and this paper audits one such failure in
depth: \textbf{wrong-advice adoption under coercion.} Under a coercive framing---high confidence,
expert-authority and accountability cues---users \emph{systematically adopt a confidently wrong AI}, a
failure dangerous even at zero between-user spread, because everyone can fail
together~\cite{bucinca2021trust,vasconcelos2023explanations,luguri2021shining}. A companion failure mode,
\emph{over-dispersion}---the interface makes reliance \emph{heterogeneous across
users}~\cite{bansal2021whole,gajos2022people}, safe for the median user and unsafe for the tails---is
developed as a diagnostic in the supplement.

The standard way to detect either failure is a human-subjects study---powered, IRB-approved, and slow.
Teams iterate on interfaces far faster than they can run such studies, so a growing practice is to screen
an interface first with a \textbf{synthetic panel}: persona-conditioned LLM agents that role-play a cohort
of users, from which the risk is estimated. Synthetic users are having a moment in
HCI~\cite{park2022social,argyle2023out,aher2023using}, and so is the backlash: a large literature warns
that LLM-simulated people are \emph{unreliable proxies}---they collapse human diversity, echo the base
model's priors, and mis-estimate distributions~\cite{santurkar2023whose,cao2025specializing,%
bisbee2024synthetic,dominguez2024questioning,cheng2023compost,hamalainen2023evaluating}. That backlash,
like the enthusiasm before it, asks whether the agents \emph{match} people. By contrast, we raise a
question that is logically prior and needs no human ground truth: swap the backend model behind the panel,
and its safety verdict on the \emph{same} interface can cross from ``safe'' to ``dangerous.''

This is no longer hypothetical. A commercial market of synthetic-user research platforms now advertises AI
``participants'' that evaluate designs \emph{in minutes} and compress (or stand in for) weeks of human
study~\cite{syntheticusers,uxia_synthetic}. A practitioner following the natural engineering defaults would
plausibly adopt two assumptions that, to our
knowledge, are never tested for interface-\emph{risk} evaluation: (i) that the \emph{strongest available}
backend is the best simulator---the natural engineering default, and exactly what a quality-optimal model
router would pick~\cite{ong2025routellm}; and (ii) that a \emph{single} backend's point estimate
(``this design's wrong-advice adoption is 0.6'') is a property of the design rather than of the model that
produced it. This paper puts both assumptions to an empirical test, and finds both wanting.

Our stance is neither boosterism nor rejection. Simulating users with LLMs is a reasonable and advanced way
to cut the cost of early evaluation, and we treat it as such. But a practitioner should not trust its
\emph{single number}. Rather than abandon the method, we show how to use it honestly: power a panel
with \emph{several capability-diverse} backends chosen to cover the failure modes a single strong model
misses, report the risk as a \emph{distribution} rather than a point estimate, and \emph{abstain}, routing
the design to a human study, exactly when the backends disagree. Cheaper than an all-human study, but not
credulous.

This reliability question has a concrete source. When a panel reports that an interface is risky, that reading
is produced not only by the interface but by a stack of choices the researcher makes \emph{freely}: which
backend LLM powers the agents, how the personas are worded, and which random generation happens to be
sampled. If those choices move the reading as much as the interface does, then a ``design risk'' number may
be as much a property of the measurement tool as of the design. Before asking whether a synthetic panel
predicts humans, we should ask whether it even \emph{measures the same thing} when a different researcher,
or the same researcher on a different day, rebuilds it. This is a question of measurement
reliability and invariance~\cite{santurkar2023whose,cheng2023compost}---logically prior to external
validity---and it is the question this paper answers. The closest published work makes the contrast
concrete: \citeauthor{seshadri2026lost}'s \emph{Lost in Simulation}~\cite{seshadri2026lost} shows that the
simulated-user backend can move an agent's \emph{success rate} by up to nine points, using human
correspondence as the yardstick; we instead ask whether the panel's own safety \emph{verdict} survives a
change of backend---reliability that must hold before any reading can be validated against people.

\emph{Scope.} Throughout, ``interface'' refers to the interface's \emph{informational content and framing}
as an agent receives it---the AI prediction, its confidence, the highlighted explanation, and the coercive
or protective wording---encoded as text (Section~\ref{sec:method}). We deliberately study this
content/framing layer, not the pixel-level visual layer (layout, salience, typography); a multimodal
rendering is future work (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Our claims concern whether a synthetic panel's reading
of \emph{that} content is backend-stable, not the visual presentation of a shipped UI.

Concretely, we audit this synthetic panel across \textbf{six backend LLMs} and \textbf{two
review-sentiment datasets} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), and ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (backend invariance).} Do the panel's interface-risk estimates---absolute wrong-advice
  adoption, the coercive-framing effect, and persona heterogeneity---agree across backends, and can a
  backend change \emph{flip} an interface from ``high risk'' to ``low risk''?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (variance decomposition).} How much of a synthetic risk estimate is attributable to the
  backend model versus the researcher-chosen items and personas versus generation randomness?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (construct vs.\ compliance).} Which panel signals reflect a stable sensitivity to the
  intended manipulation, and which primarily reflect explicit persona instructions?
  \item \textbf{RQ4 (backend-aware reporting).} Can conservative multi-model aggregation and
  disagreement-triggered abstention reduce backend-induced instability in the measurement?
\end{itemize}

Our answer separates \emph{what is stable from what is not}. \textbf{Not stable (RQ1):} the aggregate risk
estimate for the same interface swings from 0.12 to 0.62 across backends (Figure~\ref{fig:flip})---a
range wide enough that, depending on where the operating threshold sits, some backends flag the interface
while others clear it (Section~\ref{sec:instability}); a matched contrast that holds the wrong advice constant and
varies only the framing confirms a real coercive-framing effect (pooled item-clustered odds ratio 1.90 on
one dataset, a weaker 1.17 on the other; a non-coercive placebo at or below baseline; Section~\ref{sec:coercion}),
of a magnitude we cannot distinguish across backends---the backend moves the adoption \emph{level}, while
any effect on the framing slope is below what our data resolve. \textbf{Stable, but for an unglamorous reason (RQ3):} the panel faithfully
propagates a \emph{prompted} disposition---an explicitly trusting-novice persona is the highest-adopting
persona in all 12 backend$\times$dataset cells (item-clustered odds ratio $\approx$17;
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap})---which is a reliability property of the simulation, not a discovered
vulnerability, since the persona is defined to be trusting; and its cross-vendor \emph{ordering} agreement
is largely carried by this one persona (Section~\ref{sec:manipcheck}). \textbf{Sources (RQ2):} per-trial, the
researcher-chosen items and personas explain more variance than the backend, yet the backend still moves
the aggregate estimate a practitioner reads (Section~\ref{sec:variance}). We use these to motivate a
backend-aware reporting protocol (RQ4). \textbf{Most consequential:} backend capability and reproduced
vulnerability are \emph{mismatched}---the highest-capability backend least reproduces the highest-severity
failure, so the single frontier model a quality-optimal router would choose surfaces almost none of the
risk that a pair of weaker backends recovers (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}); selecting a panel for
\emph{vulnerability coverage} is therefore the inverse of capability routing.

Our \emph{claimed} contribution is the audit, not a validated screen. We contribute:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A systematic backend-sensitivity audit} of synthetic-user interface-risk measurement (six
  backends $\times$ two datasets), organized around a \emph{risk-classification flip rate}: the fraction of
  backend pairs that disagree on whether the \emph{same} interface crosses a decision threshold. Prior
  prompt/model-sensitivity work shows outputs shift in \emph{magnitude}; the flip rate recasts that shift as
  a change in a pass/fail verdict, which is what makes measurement non-invariance consequential for a
  deployment gate.
  \item \textbf{The capability--vulnerability mismatch and a synthetic-vulnerability-coverage principle for panel
  selection.} Because the strongest backend least reproduces the highest-severity failure (the at-risk
  trusting-novice persona), assembling an evaluation panel for \emph{synthetic-vulnerability coverage} is the
  \emph{inverse} of capability routing~\cite{ong2025routellm}---a counterintuitive,
  actionable principle for who should power a synthetic-user study.
  \item \textbf{A backend-aware reporting protocol on a preregistered, audit-gated pipeline}: a variance
  decomposition over backend, persona, item, and generation; a behavior taxonomy; a cross-backend
  decision-flip analysis; and disagreement-triggered abstention---report synthetic-panel results as
  \emph{distributions over models, prompts, and generations}, not a single point estimate, and abstain when
  backends disagree. The plan is frozen in advance: the coercion axis is conflict-conditioned and validated
  against a matched neutral baseline (not against human behaviour), and the pipeline caught three
  silent-corruption bugs before they could reach a result (Section~\ref{sec:method}). A companion
  over-dispersion diagnostic is in Appendix~\ref{app:overdispersion}.
\end{enumerate}

This paper establishes the internal robustness and construct sensitivity that must precede any human
validation (Section~\ref{sec:e6}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Simulated users, and the reliability critique.} LLMs as stand-ins for human participants have
been proposed for surveys, user studies, and formative
evaluation~\cite{park2022social,argyle2023out,aher2023using}. A parallel literature judges these simulators
unreliable proxies---most directly, they collapse population diversity and regress toward majority
views~\cite{santurkar2023whose}.\footnote{The same literature reports mis-calibrated
distributions~\cite{cao2025specializing,dominguez2024questioning}, non-human response
biases~\cite{tjuatja2024llms}, failure as survey-sample replacements~\cite{bisbee2024synthetic}, and
caricatured rather than simulated subpopulations~\cite{cheng2023compost}.} Closest, and now published prior work,
\citeauthor{seshadri2026lost}~\cite{seshadri2026lost} show LLM-simulated users are unreliable proxies in
agentic evaluation---agent success varies by up to nine points across the user-simulating backend, with
systematic miscalibration and demographic gaps. We differ in object and consequence: they quantify a
task-performance \emph{magnitude} shift and treat human correspondence as ground truth, whereas we show a
shift that \emph{flips} an interface-safety verdict across a decision threshold and deliberately disclaim
human correspondence, auditing internal measurement stability instead. Relatedly,
\citeauthor{robinson2026influence}~\cite{robinson2026influence} find an LLM's task skill, persuasiveness, and
\emph{vigilance} against being misled are dissociable, corroborating our capability--vulnerability inversion
(Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}) at the LLM-agent rather than simulated-user level. We sit inside this debate but change the
target: we do not ask the panel to \emph{predict} a human distribution, we ask it to \emph{triage} an
interface, and we isolate a specific, measurable pathology---\emph{model-dependence of the triage
signal}---that the prediction framing obscures. Two recent efforts sharpen this contrast. Grounding agents
in real interviews, \citeauthor{park2024generative}~\cite{park2024generative} report LLM agents that
reproduce individuals' survey responses at 82--86\% of participants' own two-week test--retest
consistency---an \emph{accuracy/correspondence}
result; we ask the logically prior \emph{reliability} question (is the risk reading even reproducible across
backends?) that such a validation effort presupposes. \citeauthor{hu2024quantifying}~\cite{hu2024quantifying}
decompose how much annotation variance a \emph{persona} explains and find larger instruction-tuned models
simulate personas \emph{better} where persona matters; our decomposition instead isolates the \emph{backend}
as the free variable and, in the interface-risk setting, finds the \emph{opposite} for the highest-severity
persona (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln})---a domain-specific tension we name the capability--vulnerability
inversion rather than a contradiction.

\paragraph{Reliance and over-reliance on AI advice.} A large body of work studies when people
appropriately rely on AI given confidence and
explanations~\cite{bansal2021whole,bucinca2021trust,zhang2020confidence,lai2019human,schemmer2023appropriate},
often finding that explanations \emph{increase} over-reliance rather than calibrate
it~\cite{bansal2021whole}. Recent work benchmarks reliance
\emph{interventions} (disclaimers, uncertainty highlighting) with human participants on tasks including
LSAT, and finds they curb over-reliance but rarely improve \emph{appropriate}
reliance~\cite{bo2024rely}; and carefully designed explanations can \emph{reduce} rather than inflate
over-reliance~\cite{vasconcelos2023explanations}. This work treats between-user variance as noise
around an average, and studies humans directly. We invert the first: reliance \emph{heterogeneity} is our
axis-1 safety DV, and coercive wrong-AI adoption is axis-2; and we complement the second: our
protective-intervention arm (Appendix~\ref{app:protective}) asks not \emph{whether} an intervention helps
humans but \emph{whether a synthetic panel can even detect that it helps}, and how that verdict depends on
the backend.

\paragraph{Dark patterns and coercive interfaces.} Deceptive and coercive designs are well
catalogued~\cite{luguri2021shining,mildner2023dark}; our axis-2 gives a computational signal for one
coercive mode (a confidently wrong AI) and, via personas, a read on which prompted profiles the
simulation marks as susceptible.

\paragraph{Evaluating human--AI decision teams.} The closest HCI work studies cognitive bias and
complementarity in AI-assisted decisions~\cite{rastogi2022deciding}, not interface-level safety triage. The gap we fill is a \emph{pre-study screen for interface reliance safety},
and, critically, a characterization of when such a screen's signal is trustworthy.

\paragraph{Measurement invariance, reliability, and validity.} Psychometrics learned this lesson long ago:
an instrument that gives a different reading each time is \emph{unreliable} before we ever ask whether it
is \emph{valid} (whether it measures the intended construct), and \emph{measurement invariance} asks
whether it means the same thing across groups or contexts~\cite{santurkar2023whose,tjuatja2024llms}. We
import that lens with one twist: the \emph{backend LLM is part of the instrument}, so ``which model?'' is a
measurement-invariance question, not a configuration detail, and stability across generations is a
reliability question. The estimand
we track is a \emph{specification sensitivity}---how far an interface-risk reading moves across alternative,
equally-defensible panel implementations---rather than invariance of one fixed latent construct across
pre-set groups. Stability does
not imply validity, but severe backend non-invariance undermines any validity claim built on a single
backend~\cite{dominguez2024questioning}.

\paragraph{Researcher degrees of freedom in LLM evaluation.} Model choice, prompt wording, decoding
settings, output parsing, persona phrasing, and sample count are analytic choices that can each move a
reported result---a form of hidden flexibility analogous to the ``garden of forking paths'' and
undisclosed analytic flexibility in empirical research~\cite{simmons2011false}. In LLM evaluation
specifically, small prompt-format changes swing results~\cite{sclar2024quantifying} and single-prompt
scores misrepresent ability~\cite{mizrahi2024state}. A generalizability-theory decomposition of LLM output
variance into model, prompt, and sampling components has been applied to creative
tasks~\cite{haase2026within}. We make these degrees of freedom the object of study,
and adopt specification-curve-style multiverse reporting~\cite{simonsohn2020specification} rather than a
single spec. Our decomposition targets a different quantity than this prior work---not the magnitude or
quality of an output, but whether a \emph{binary interface-risk verdict} survives the researcher's free
choices.

\paragraph{Algorithmic auditing and robustness reporting.} Our stance---stress-test a widely-adopted
measurement practice, quantify its sensitivity, and recommend distributional reporting with
abstention---follows the tradition of sensitivity analysis, model/evaluation cards~\cite{mitchell2019model},
and multi-model benchmarking with reliability-aware judging~\cite{zheng2023judging}. We contribute a
concrete audit protocol for synthetic-user interface evaluation. Our novelty relative to this body of work
is the \emph{object} and the \emph{consequence}: prior prompt/model-sensitivity results are about accuracy
or benchmark \emph{scores}, whereas we show that the same free choices move an interface-level \emph{safety
decision} in the synthetic-user HCI evaluation setting---flipping whether a design is flagged for a human
study---which is the point at which such measurement instability actually matters for design practice.

\section{Two Ways a Design Fails, Made Measurable}
\label{sec:definitions}

We characterize an interface on two axes, both measured on the panel's decisions. The coercion axis
(axis~2) is the load-bearing quantity we audit in the main paper; the over-dispersion axis (axis~1) is
developed as a companion diagnostic in the supplement (Appendix~\ref{app:overdispersion}).

\paragraph{Axis 1---between-user reliance over-dispersion.} Intuitively: does the interface treat all users
alike, or split them? A design that is safe for the median user but reckless for the tail shows up as a
wide \emph{spread} of reliance between users. For each item, personas produce reliance decisions; we
estimate the \emph{excess variance} beyond the binomial $p(1-p)$ a single homogeneous user would
produce---the \emph{over-dispersion}, a beta-binomial parameter $\rho$. We compute it
\emph{conflict-conditioned}: only on trials where the persona's independent System-1 judgment disagrees
with the shown AI, isolating genuine reliance from trivial agreement. As a stable, bounded summary that
avoids the beta-binomial estimator's boundary behaviour at extreme rates (Section~\ref{sec:method}), we
report \emph{mean panel disagreement}: for a condition with personas $j=1,\dots,P$ ($P=6$), let $r_j$ be
persona $j$'s conflict-conditioned reliance rate; the condition's disagreement is their between-persona
(sample) variance
\begin{equation}
D=\frac{1}{P-1}\sum_{j=1}^{P}\bigl(r_j-\bar r\bigr)^2,\qquad \bar r=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{j=1}^{P} r_j,
\end{equation}
and we report the mean of $D$ over the five UI conditions. $D$ tracks $\rho$ monotonically and is what we
plot; we report $\rho$ alongside it. High axis-1 means the panel estimates reliance as persona-sensitive---a
candidate signal of user-sensitive risk, not yet human-validated.

\paragraph{Axis 2---wrong-advice adoption under coercion.} Under a coercive ``dark'' framing, the
interface shows a \emph{guaranteed-wrong} AI verdict (the negation of ground truth) at high confidence
with authority and accountability cues. Axis-2 is the rate at which personas adopt it. To separate the
\emph{framing} from ordinary wrong-advice following, we contrast it against neutral and placebo framings
on matched items (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}). It is dangerous even at zero dispersion---a uniform failure.

\paragraph{From axes to a decision, and why we audit it.} Eventually a screen has to \emph{decide}: flag
this design for a human study, or wave it through. That decision---not the raw axis value---is what a team
acts on, so it is what we put under audit. We deliberately do \emph{not}
propose or validate a full rule here; we take the simplest version (a threshold on axis-2 adoption) as the
\emph{object of the audit}, and ask whether the decision it produces is stable across backends
(Section~\ref{sec:instability}). Any thresholds remain unfrozen: their calibration and validation depend on
the human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}).

\section{Method: The Synthetic Panel}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Panel $=$ personas $\times$ backends.} Each agent is a persona (a vector of domain skill, AI
literacy, risk sensitivity, caution) crossed with a base LLM (the ``backend''). Personas span
novice/expert $\times$ trusting/skeptical plus two moderates (six in total). A persona's prompt
is generated identically across backends and its decoding temperature is fixed \emph{per persona}, not per
backend, so temperature and wording are held constant when we vary the backend. Backend effects are
therefore not confounded with per-model prompt or temperature differences.

\paragraph{Pre- and post-advice decisions.} Each agent first produces a judgment \emph{without} AI (a
frozen ``System-1''/pre-advice anchor), then a decision \emph{with} the AI advice and framing (``System-2''/
post-advice). We use the System-1/System-2 labels descriptively, not as a validated model of dual-process
cognition. Reliance is defined against the pre-advice anchor---what makes conflict-conditioning possible and
prevents the ``everyone-agrees-so-everyone-complies'' collapse of a naive panel.

\paragraph{Textual prompt encoding of the interface.} Agents receive a \emph{textual serialization} of
each interface condition---not screenshots and not a vision model. The AI prediction, its confidence, and
(for the explanation conditions) the highlighted tokens are rendered as text in the prompt, following the
Bansal et al.~\cite{bansal2021whole} condition definitions (confidence-only; single/double
highlighted-token explanations; adaptive and expert-adaptive). The dark condition negates ground truth and
adds high confidence plus authority/accountability language; a placebo condition presents the real AI
non-coercively. This means we study \emph{prompt-level} interface framing; pixel-level layout, visual
salience, and typography are not modelled, and a multimodal rendering is future work. The exact per-model
prompt templates are in the supplement.

\paragraph{Stimuli and datasets.} Items are drawn from the Bansal sentiment benchmark, held out from any
threshold fitting, and selected to concentrate on loci of human reliance heterogeneity (disclosed; human
outcomes are used only for \emph{item selection}, never leaked into agent prompts---item selection $\ne$
label leakage). We run two \emph{review-sentiment datasets}: \textbf{beer reviews} and \textbf{Amazon book
reviews (amzbook)}. Both are English binary sentiment, so we treat this as replication across two
datasets within one task family, not broad cross-domain generalization. A preregistered LSAT arm
(Appendix~\ref{app:lsat}) tests whether the core findings survive a different task structure
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Backends.} A within-provider series (gpt-4o-mini, gpt-4.1, gpt-4o, gpt-5.5, identical config)
plus independent-vendor models (claude-sonnet-4.5, gemini-2.5-pro): six backends total. We order the
same-provider series by release for presentation only and make no claim that this is a validated capability
scale; ``frontier'' refers to the most recent, most capable general model at the time of writing (gpt-5.5).

\paragraph{Rigor spine and process integrity.} Before running each arm we froze the backend set, persona
set, item-selection seeds, conditions, primary DVs, and the predicted direction of each effect, in a
UTC-timestamped, committed record. Deviations and post-hoc analyses (e.g.\ the exploratory threshold sweep
and the descriptor ablation) are logged and labelled as such. Correspondence analyses were coded blind;
every merge was gated on an independent audit \emph{and} a from-raw numeric re-derivation. This spine caught
three silent-corruption bugs that would each have produced a plausible-but-false result: an axis-2
\emph{sign inversion} (scoring adoption against \texttt{ground\_truth} instead of the displayed
$1-\texttt{ground\_truth}$), a beta-binomial \emph{boundary artifact}, and a decision parser that silently
\emph{fabricated} a $(\text{decision}{=}0,\text{conf}{=}0.5)$ sentinel on parse failure. It also caught a
statistical error in our own draft---a generalized estimating equation (GEE) interaction test that appeared to show backend-dependent framing
but was an artifact of few item clusters (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}). We report these openly: a pipeline
that catches its own errors is what makes the numbers trustworthy.

\section{Experiments and Results}

We report per-backend (no pooling across incomparable backends), with effect sizes, confidence intervals,
and Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) correction. Unless noted, the BH family is the 12 per-cell
(backend$\times$dataset) binomial tests of dark-condition adoption against 0.5. There are $n=300$
dark-condition trials per backend$\times$dataset cell (6 personas $\times$ 50 items).

\emph{Roadmap of the results.} We show the synthetic
\emph{reading} of an interface's wrong-advice risk is \emph{not} stable across backends (Sections~\ref{sec:modeldep}--\ref{sec:instability});
we locate where that instability comes from (Section~\ref{sec:variance}), and find that the strongest
backend is the blindest to the most at-risk user (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}). Two boundary tests---a
structurally different task and a protective-design comparison---are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:lsat}
and~\ref{app:protective}. To keep the hierarchy legible we flag each subsection inline with one of
two tiers: \textbf{primary} confirmatory audit results (RQ1: backend non-invariance, the matched
coercive-framing contrast, and decision instability); and \textbf{mechanism} analyses (RQ2--RQ3: where the
instability lives, the manipulation check, and the capability--vulnerability mismatch).


\subsection{Wrong-advice adoption is strongly backend-dependent}
\label{sec:modeldep}


\textbf{[Primary (RQ1).]} Start with the first number a practitioner reads off: how often the panel adopts a wrong AI. Swap the model
behind the panel and both the \emph{level} and the \emph{interpretation} of that number change
(Figure~\ref{fig:flip}). Across the ladder (beer / amzbook): gpt-4o-mini 0.42 / 0.41, gpt-4.1
\textbf{0.55 / 0.62}, gpt-4o 0.42 / 0.39, gpt-5.5 \textbf{0.20 / 0.12}. The adoption peak at gpt-4.1 is
real (between-model Fisher vs.\ gpt-4o $p=0.001$ beer, $p<10^{-4}$ amzbook) but \textbf{model-idiosyncratic}:
the smallest ladder model sits below 0.5, and after BH correction gpt-4.1's elevation above chance survives
on amzbook ($p<0.001$) but is only nominal on beer (BH $p=0.13$). Independent-vendor models are at or below
chance (claude 0.48, gemini 0.47 on beer); indeed no backend clears 0.5 significantly on beer, and only
gpt-4.1 does on amzbook. Rather than interpret
adoption against a $0.5$ ``chance'' baseline---which is not a natural no-effect point, since personas are
not random guessers---we ground the effect in a matched framing contrast below.

The panel's internal persona disagreement is likewise backend-dependent; we report it in the supplement (Appendix~\ref{app:overdispersion}).

But is that adoption really about
\emph{coercion}, or just ordinary wrong-advice following? We settle that next, then return to whether the
model-specificity actually flips a screening decision.

\subsection{Is it coercion, or just wrong-advice following? A matched framing contrast}
\label{sec:coercion}

\textbf{[Primary (RQ1).]} Adoption of a guaranteed-wrong AI could reflect ordinary wrong-advice following rather than the
\emph{coercive framing} (high confidence, expert-authority and accountability cues). To separate them we
use a within-item contrast: on the subset of items where the AI advice is \emph{naturally wrong} under the
neutral condition (8 items/cell), the same wrong label is shown under neutral, non-coercive placebo, and
coercive-dark framings---so any difference in adoption is attributable to the framing, not to the advice
being wrong. We fit a logistic model \texttt{adopt $\sim$ dark $+$ placebo $+$ model} (clustered by item)
for the pooled framing effect, and test backend-dependence with a likelihood-ratio test of the
\texttt{dark$\times$model} interaction.

The coercive framing has a real effect. Pooled across backends, the dark framing raises wrong-advice
adoption over the matched neutral baseline with an odds ratio of \textbf{1.90} on beer (95\% CI 1.42--2.53),
while the non-coercive placebo stays at or below neutral (beer OR 0.94, n.s.; amzbook OR 0.88, $p=0.005$, a
small \emph{protective} effect)---so the driver is the coercion, not mere AI-presence. The beer effect is
robust across specifications; on
amzbook it is weaker and directional (OR 1.17, 95\% CI 1.04--1.31).\footnote{Re-estimating the pooled OR
with few-cluster-valid methods (only eight matched-wrong item clusters): item random-intercept (beer 2.02,
amzbook 1.22, CI 0.93--1.61), item fixed effects (beer 1.92), and an item-cluster bootstrap (beer 1.93).
Beer excludes 1 under all three; amzbook crosses 1 under the random-intercept model.} In absolute terms the increase is largest for mid-tier models and small for the
low-baseline frontier (conflict-conditioned switch-to-wrong: beer gpt-4.1 $0.19\to0.51$, gpt-4o
$0.12\to0.29$, gpt-5.5 $0.04\to0.10$). We do not claim the framing effect \emph{itself} varies by backend---
the \texttt{dark$\times$model} interaction is non-significant on both datasets (likelihood-ratio
$\chi^2(5)=1.70$, $p=0.89$ beer; $\chi^2(5)=3.18$, $p=0.67$ amzbook)---because what the backend strongly
moves is the absolute adoption \emph{level} (Section~\ref{sec:instability}), not the framing slope. The
cues here are \emph{bundled} (confidence, authority, and accountability co-vary and are not separated).
Grounding adoption in this matched neutral condition also retires the arbitrary $0.5$ baseline: the
frontier's low adoption is a low \emph{level}, measured against neutral rather than against chance.

\subsection{Cross-backend decision instability: the same interface, flipped}
\label{sec:instability}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_decision_flip}
  \caption{The \emph{same} guaranteed-wrong interface, scored by six backends, both datasets. Dots are
  aggregate wrong-advice adoption with Wilson 95\% binomial CIs; the dashed line is a fixed decision
  threshold ($\tau=0.5$). Backends at or above threshold would \emph{flag} the interface (red); the rest
  \emph{clear} it (blue)---so the screening decision flips with the backend (pairwise flip rate 0.33 on both
  datasets). Several mid-adoption backends---including the one flagging backend---have CIs that straddle
  $\tau$: the flip rate measures
  decision instability \emph{across} backends, not a claim that each individual crossing is significant---
  the threshold-free companions (adoption range 0.12--0.62, Fleiss'~$\kappa$) carry the same reading
  without fixing $\tau$.}
  \label{fig:flip}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{[Primary (RQ1).]} If the aggregate reading is this model-specific, does the \emph{decision} a screen would make move with it?
We treat the dark interface as one \emph{design} and ask whether the six backends agree on its risk
(Figure~\ref{fig:flip}). They do not. Aggregate wrong-advice adoption for the identical
interface is 0.20--0.55
across the six beer backends and 0.12--0.62 across the amzbook backends---a spread of 0.35 and 0.50 on a
0--1 scale. This range is the threshold-free heart of the non-invariance: whatever operating threshold
$\tau$ a team picks in the plausible band ($0.35$--$0.55$ beer, $0.30$--$0.62$ amzbook), some backends land
above it and others below, so no threshold in that band makes the six backends agree. Turning the estimate
into the binary decision a screen would make---``flag for a human study if adoption $\ge \tau$''---therefore
inherits that instability. At $\tau=0.5$ one backend (gpt-4.1) flags each interface and the other five clear
it: a \textbf{pairwise backend flip rate}---the fraction of backend pairs that disagree on the flag---of
0.33 on both datasets. But this point count is itself fragile: gpt-4.1's Wilson interval reaches below 0.5
and several clearing backends' intervals reach above it (Figure~\ref{fig:flip}), which is exactly why we
rest the non-invariance claim on the \emph{range} rather than on a lucky threshold. Sweeping $\tau$ across
its full range makes this explicit (Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}): the pairwise flip rate stays positive across
the entire plausible band---and well beyond it---so the disagreement is a property of the backends, not an
artifact of the single $\tau=0.5$ operating point we plot in Figure~\ref{fig:flip}. We therefore read any
single-$\tau$ flip rate as \emph{decision instability conditional on whatever operating threshold a team
adopts}---not as a calibrated safety classifier, since $\tau$ itself is not validated against human harm.
Two threshold-free companions tell the same story without fixing $\tau$: the raw adoption range 0.12--0.62
above, and the persona-level risk flags, which agree only moderately across
backends (Fleiss' $\kappa=0.55$ beer, $0.45$ amzbook). In measurement terms, the instrument is not
invariant: swapping the backend can move an interface from ``high risk'' to ``low risk'' without touching
the design. A behavior audit rules out one benign explanation---that low-adopting backends simply
\emph{refuse} the deceptive task: explicit challenge/refusal language is rare (0\% of dark responses for
the OpenAI backends, 2--4\% for claude and gemini), and low adoption is achieved by evidence-based
re-verification, not by declining to answer.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_threshold_sweep}
  \caption{Backend disagreement is not an artifact of the $\tau=0.5$ operating point. Sweeping the decision
  threshold, the pairwise backend flip rate stays positive across the whole plausible band on both
  datasets (nonzero for $\tau\in[0.20,0.55]$ beer and $[0.13,0.62]$ amzbook), peaking near $\tau\approx0.43$.
  The $\tau=0.5$ we plot elsewhere (dotted line) is one operating point on a wide plateau, not a knife-edge.}
  \label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Everything the analyst chooses moves the number}
\label{sec:variance}
\textbf{[Mechanism (RQ2).]} So who is really moving the number---the model, or the researcher? A variance decomposition gives an
uncomfortable answer: \emph{both}, and the researcher more. A crossed random-effects model of per-trial adoption ($\text{adopt}\sim 1+(1|\text{backend})+(1|\text{persona})
+(1|\text{item})$) apportions variance \emph{more} to the researcher-chosen items and personas than to the
backend (approximate variance shares: item $\approx$0.28, persona $\approx$0.23, backend $\approx$0.07,
residual $\approx$0.42; between-group SDs 0.16/0.25/0.13 corroborate the ordering)---which sharpens rather
than contradicts the decision flips above: the backend is a modest source of \emph{per-trial} noise but
still shifts the \emph{aggregate} estimate a practitioner reads enough to flip the screen. The practical implication is the same for
every source: item selection, persona wording, and backend are all analyst choices that move the reading,
so researchers should report a synthetic-panel result as a \emph{distribution} over these choices rather
than a single point estimate. Generation randomness---the one source a single draw per prompt cannot expose---turns out to be
the smallest of all. In a preregistered multi-generation run (three backends spanning the adoption range
$\times$ two UI conditions $\times$ six personas $\times$ 20 items $\times$ five generation seeds; 3{,}600 trials), generation
contributes essentially none of the variance in dark adoption (fixed-effects $\eta^2=0.001$, versus $0.21$
for items, $0.15$ for personas, and $0.07$ for the backend; Figure~\ref{fig:variance}; a separate
decomposition of this smaller run). Each backend's aggregate over-reliance
estimate moves by at most $\text{SD}=0.03$ across the five seeds (flip-metric range $\le0.09$), small
against the $0.31$ spread \emph{between} backends that flips the decision. Generation noise is real at the
level of an individual persona--item cell (mean cell SD $0.17$; about a third of cells change across seeds),
but it averages out: two-thirds of cells are reproduced identically. The aggregate reading a
practitioner acts on is therefore stable across generations, and the backend and persona effects above are not
artifacts of a lucky draw.\footnote{We report a fixed-effects $\eta^2$ share because a random-effects
variance component for the backend is degenerate on three groups; the direct stability statistics are the
primary, model-free evidence.}

\subsection{A backend-stable manipulation check: the panel propagates a prompted trust disposition}
\label{sec:manipcheck}

  multi-generation run (three backends $\times$ two UI conditions $\times$ six personas $\times$ 20 items
  $\times$ five generation seeds; 3{,}600 trials). The analyst-chosen item and persona dominate; the backend

  backend share still shifts the aggregate estimate enough to flip the decision
  (Section~\ref{sec:instability}).}
  \label{fig:variance}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2_persona_heatmap}
  \caption{Persona $\times$ model wrong-advice adoption on the dark condition, both datasets. An
  explicitly trusting-novice persona (p5, highlighted) is the highest-adopting persona in all 12

  \label{fig:heatmap}

\textbf{[Mechanism (RQ3): manipulation check.]} Is anything about the panel stable? One thing is. The aggregate adoption \emph{rate} is model-idiosyncratic
(range 0.35 across beer backends, 0.50 across amzbook backends), but the \emph{ordering} of which persona
adopts most holds steady (Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}): an \textbf{explicitly trusting-novice} persona (p5) is
the strict top adopter in \textbf{all 12} backend$\times$dataset cells. Because items are reused across
personas and the 12 cells share prompts, items, and providers, we do not treat trials as independent; a
crossed random-effects logistic model with item, backend, and dataset random effects, together with an
item-clustered GEE, gives a large odds ratio ($\approx$17, 95\% CI 14--20) for that persona relative to the
others---though with p5 near the ceiling in most cells, the model-free ranking rather than the OR
\emph{magnitude} is the evidence. We read this as a manipulation check the panel \emph{passes}, not as a
headline discovery: p5 is \emph{prompt-defined} to trust, so ``it adopts the AI most'' demonstrates
faithful, stable propagation of a prompted disposition---a reliability property of the simulation, not a
discovered human vulnerability. Beneath that shared peak the vendors agree less: mean pairwise Spearman
across independent vendors falls to 0.73 on beer and 0.38 on amzbook once p5 is excluded (0.85 and 0.66
with it), and the ordering detail, a background-only descriptor ablation, and a non-policy dispositional
control---which shows the effect survives with no trust instruction given at all---are in
Appendix~\ref{app:manip}.

\subsection{Capability--vulnerability mismatch: strong backends under-represent the at-risk user}
\label{sec:capvuln}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig6_capability_vulnerability}
  \caption{\textbf{The frontier backend under-represents the at-risk user.} \emph{Left:} backend task
  competence (System-1 accuracy) vs.\ the AI-induced flip rate
  $P(\text{adopt}\mid\text{System-1 correct})$, both datasets---a directional negative trend (Spearman
  $-0.26$ / $-0.54$; n.s.\ at $n=6$, so the fit is a visual guide, not the load-bearing evidence).
  \emph{Right:} vulnerability coverage (number of the six personas whose
  dark adoption reaches 0.5) for a single frontier backend, a weak pair, and the full panel---the model-free
  reading the argument rests on: the single frontier backend surfaces the fewest at-risk profiles.}
  \label{fig:capvuln}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{[Mechanism: capability--vulnerability.]} Now the study's most unsettling turn. A backend's aggregate adoption is one thing; \emph{which} users it
flags as at-risk is another---and combining the two exposes an uncomfortable pattern
(Figure~\ref{fig:capvuln}). Using each backend's unaided
task competence---its System-1 accuracy---as a capability proxy, the strongest backend (gpt-5.5, 0.94) is
precisely the one that least reproduces the highest-severity failure: on the trusting-novice persona (p5),
dark-condition adoption is $\approx$1.0 for five backends but only \textbf{0.42} for gpt-5.5 (0.54 beer,
0.30 amzbook). Isolating AI
influence from baseline error---the \emph{AI-induced flip}, $P(\text{adopt}\mid\text{System-1 correct})$,
which excludes trials where the persona was already wrong (there adoption is 1 by construction)---the same
ordering holds: the frontier flips a correct answer to the wrong AI least often (0.11), a mid-tier backend
most (0.44). In short, \emph{you would not crash-test with your safest driver}---yet a synthetic panel
powered by the frontier model does exactly that, screening for over-reliance danger with the one ``user''
least capable of being over-reliant.

\paragraph{The pattern sharpens at $n=11$ backends.} It is not an artifact of the six-model ladder.
Widening to \textbf{eleven} backends across three vendors and a broad capability range, and pooling both
datasets, capability anti-correlates with all three vulnerability measures (Spearman: aggregate adoption
$-0.82$, AI-induced flip $-0.67$, p5 adoption $-0.65$; Benjamini--Hochberg $p\le0.03$;
Figure~\ref{fig:expand11}).\footnote{Added backends: gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4, gpt-5.4, claude-opus-4.6, and
gemini-3.1-pro; a twelfth, claude-haiku-4.5, is excluded because it never emitted a parseable decision---an
instrument failure, disclosed.} The aggregate-adoption anti-correlation is the sturdy one---significant in
each dataset ($-0.68$ / $-0.98$) and every leave-one-backend-out jackknife; the flip and p5 correlations are
amzbook-carried and single-backend fragile. But the reading does not hang on the correlation:
\textbf{the frontier backend is nearly blind to the at-risk persona.}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig6b_expand11}
  \caption{Backend expansion to $n=11$ (pooling both datasets). Left: backend task competence
  (System-1 accuracy) vs.\ three dark-condition vulnerability measures, tiered by robustness---aggregate
  adoption (filled, solid fit) is jackknife-robust and significant in each dataset; AI-induced flip and p5
  adoption (open, dashed fit) are BH-significant only pooled and are amzbook-carried / single-backend
  fragile. Right: the $n=11$ vulnerability-coverage curve---a capability-first (router) panel reaches full
  coverage of the high-risk cells only at $k=10$, while a coverage-greedy panel is complete almost
  immediately; a single frontier backend covers $1/9$.}
  \label{fig:expand11}
\end{figure*}

This has a concrete consequence for assembling an evaluation panel. Treating a persona as \emph{covered}
when its dark adoption reaches a risk threshold (0.5), a single frontier backend---the model a
quality-optimal router~\cite{ong2025routellm} would select---surfaces the risk for only
1 of 6 personas on beer and 0 of 6 on amzbook, whereas a pair of weaker backends (gpt-4.1, gpt-4o-mini)
surfaces 4 and 3 (matching the full six-backend panel). Coverage is monotone in panel size by construction,
so the point is not ``more models is better''; it is that the \emph{default best choice is the worst one
for this purpose}. Choosing an evaluation panel to maximize \textbf{synthetic-vulnerability coverage} is
therefore the \emph{inverse} of choosing it to maximize task quality: a practice built on ``use the
strongest model'' systematically misses the failure cells that matter most.

We can make this selection principle precise. Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of high-severity cells
(dataset$\times$persona; high-severity by the \emph{panel's own} adoption)
and $a_b(c)$ backend $b$'s dark adoption on cell $c$; a panel $S$ of backends
\emph{covers} every cell that any of its members reproduces above a risk threshold $\tau$, with coverage
\begin{equation}
f(S)=\bigl|\{\,c\in\mathcal{C}:\ \textstyle\max_{b\in S} a_b(c)\ge\tau\,\}\bigr|.
\end{equation}
$f$ is monotone and submodular, so the \emph{coverage-greedy} panel---$S_{k+1}=S_k\cup\{\arg\max_{b}
f(S_k\cup\{b\})\}$---carries the standard $(1-1/e)$ approximation guarantee against the best size-$k$ panel.
Figure~\ref{fig:expand11} (right) shows this contrast at $n{=}11$; the same $n{=}6$ comparison of a
\emph{capability-first} panel that adds
backends strongest-first, i.e.\ the order a quality-optimal router~\cite{ong2025routellm}
would prefer, is almost opposite to the coverage-greedy panel. On the clean AI-induced-flip metric the greedy panel covers every
high-risk cell with two backends, whereas the capability-first panel's first pick---the frontier model---
covers a single cell and only reaches full coverage at $k=6$. Under the raw-adoption metric the contrast is
starker still: one \emph{low}-capability backend (gpt-4.1) already covers all seven high-risk cells, so the
single best probe is the weakest model and the router's frontier pick is the worst. Selecting a panel for
vulnerability coverage is thus not merely different from capability routing---it is close to its inverse.

A caveat is built into this metric, and we state it plainly: a cell counts as high-severity by the panel's
own adoption, and a backend counts as covering it by that same adoption, so higher-adopting backends cover
more \emph{by construction}. Coverage is a \emph{sensitivity} construct, not a validity one---at the limit,
a backend that adopts everything has perfect coverage and no discriminative value. What is \emph{not}
definitional is \emph{why} the weaker models adopt more: capability and adoption anti-correlate
empirically, so the router's default---the strongest model---is the \emph{least sensitive} prober.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_specificity}
  \caption{Coverage buys sensitivity with specificity. For each backend, wrong-advice adoption on the
  coercive (dark) interface---the sensitivity that coverage rewards---plotted against wrong-advice adoption
  on the \emph{non}-coercive (neutral) interface, a false-flag rate on a design that carries no dark
  pattern. The two track each other tightly (Spearman $0.86$): the same high-coverage backends (gpt-4.1)
  also over-adopt on safe interfaces, while the frontier backend (gpt-5.5) is low on both. Points sit above
  the diagonal, so coercion does add signal beyond baseline credulity---but a backend that ``flags
  everything'' would sit \emph{on} it, which is why coverage alone cannot certify an instrument.}
  \label{fig:specificity}
\end{figure}

This trade-off is measurable, and it guards against the obvious failure mode: a backend that simply adopts
every wrong AI would post perfect coverage. It does not come free. The backends that cover the most
dark cells are also the ones that adopt the most wrong advice on the \emph{non}-coercive (neutral)
interface---a false alarm, since that design carries no dark pattern---with a Spearman of $0.86$ between the
two (Figure~\ref{fig:specificity}). Coverage therefore buys sensitivity at the cost of specificity, exactly
as a screening trade-off should; the coercion-specific signal (dark minus neutral adoption) stays positive
for every backend, so the dark pattern does add signal beyond baseline credulity. Read correctly, then, the
selection principle is a \emph{conservative} screening-design choice---favour sensitivity when a missed
at-risk cell costs more than a false alarm---not a claim that credulous backends are simply better;
whether higher sensitivity improves screening against real users is what the planned study
(Section~\ref{sec:e6}) must decide.

The capability--vulnerability inversion is also not merely mechanical. A more-accurate backend cannot adopt
a wrong AI on a trial it already answers correctly, so higher System-1 accuracy mechanically depresses raw
adoption. To remove that advantage we restrict to the cells where \emph{all six} backends' System-1 answer
is correct (beer $n{=}133$, amzbook $n{=}131$ item$\times$persona cells) and recompute adoption there: the
frontier backend still adopts far less (gpt-5.5 $0.06$--$0.08$) than the weaker models ($0.24$--$0.42$), so
a robust residual inversion survives accuracy matching. Conditioning the AI-induced flip on a correct prior
tells the same story (gpt-5.5 $0.14$/$0.08$ vs.\ gpt-4.1 $0.43$/$0.46$). The mechanical component explains
part of the raw gap, not the direction: even head-to-head on identical, already-correct trials, the
strongest backend is the least likely to be talked out of a right answer by a coercive interface.

The same mismatch appears in the vocabulary of appropriate reliance~\cite{schemmer2023appropriate}: in the
field's own terms the frontier backend is the \emph{most appropriately reliant} user---it has the highest
relative self-reliance, keeping its own correct answer when the AI is wrong, and the lowest over-reliance
under the adversarial dark condition---which is precisely what makes it the least useful probe for an
interface whose danger \emph{is} over-reliance. Capability trends with appropriate reliance across the six
backends but is not significant at $n=6$, so we read it as directional support for what the coverage result
shows directly (Appendix~\ref{app:reliance}).

The ordering, and the inversion that produces it, also replicate on a structurally different LSAT
logical-reasoning task---4-way multiple choice, harder, and a different advice format
(Appendix~\ref{app:lsat}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Power the panel for coverage, not capability.} Use several capability-diverse

(illustratively, range $>0.2$), decline to certify and route the design to a human study.

effect \emph{across} backends and treat a single-backend ``it helps''/``it doesn't'' as

\end{enumerate}

\label{fig:protocol}

\paragraph{The backend is part of the measurement instrument.} A synthetic panel does not simply reveal
an interface's risk; it co-produces that reading with the backend, prompt, and generation the analyst
chooses. Changing the backend therefore changes the instrument. When that choice flips the interface's
risk classification (Section~\ref{sec:instability}), a single-backend ``design risk'' number is not yet a
property of the design. The question ``which LLM should power my synthetic study?'' is not an implementation
detail; it is a first-order methodological decision.

\paragraph{Capability is not validity.} Choosing the strongest model as the best simulator is tempting,
but our frontier model follows a coercively-framed wrong AI least. That may make it a better
\emph{task-solver}; it does not establish that it better tracks the target users---you would not
crash-test with your safest driver. Higher capability can even compress the persona differentiation on
which the screen relies. Without a human criterion, capability and correctness cannot justify the choice of
synthetic participant. Nor do our results show that the frontier model ``misses real danger'' or that
weaker models are ``more human''---either claim requires the human study we have not run.

\paragraph{Disagreement should trigger abstention, not averaging.} Cross-backend disagreement is not merely
measurement error. It identifies the interfaces for which a single-backend verdict is least trustworthy.
Treating that disagreement as information turns our reporting protocol (Section~\ref{sec:variance}) from
general caution into an actionable rule: when the instruments disagree, the panel should decline to certify
(Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} collects these steps into a single workflow).

\paragraph{Three kinds of failure.} Our evidence separates (i) an \emph{interface-level synthetic signal}
(agents show adoption or disagreement), from (ii) a \emph{measurement failure} (the signal flips when the
backend or prompt changes), from (iii) a \emph{human-facing design failure} (real users are harmed). This
paper establishes (i) and characterizes (ii); (iii) is reserved for the human study.

\paragraph{Implications for commercial synthetic-user tooling.} Our findings speak directly to two
assumptions that a synthetic-user platform following standard engineering defaults would plausibly
adopt~\cite{syntheticusers,uxia_synthetic}. \emph{First, rethink
``frontier-first.''} The instinct to power a screen with the strongest available model---the choice a
quality-optimal router makes~\cite{ong2025routellm}---is close to the worst one for a risk
audit: the strongest backend least reproduces the highest-severity failure (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}), so
a risk panel should be assembled for \emph{synthetic-vulnerability} coverage, which can require deliberately including
weaker or more diverse backends: a \emph{single} frontier backend covers almost none of the risk that a
weak pair recovers (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}). \emph{Second, retire the single point
estimate.} A one-backend number (``wrong-advice adoption $=0.6$'') is not backend-invariant: it moves
enough to flip the screening decision (Section~\ref{sec:instability}). Like a weather forecast, researchers
should report a synthetic-panel result as a \emph{distribution} over backends, prompts, and generations,
with a disagreement-triggered \emph{abstention}: when backends disagree beyond a preset margin (here,
illustratively and not yet calibrated, a range exceeding $0.2$), the tool should decline to certify and route the design to human testing rather than
emit a safe/unsafe verdict. \emph{Third, prefer relative to absolute claims.} Even for the comparative
question such tools are usually asked---``does this safeguard help?''---the \emph{direction} of the
improvement is backend-robust while its magnitude, and on one backend its very presence, is not, so a
single-backend ``it helps''/``it doesn't'' is provisional (Appendix~\ref{app:protective}). None of this
replaces a human study; it makes an honest
synthetic screen a \emph{triage} step that knows when to defer to one.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic personas are not people}---the central threat. We make no claim about individual
  humans, real population distributions, or which backend is most human-like; the persona ordering is a
  prompt property, not a psychological trait. Only the human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}) grounds these.
  \item \textbf{The measurement is not backend-invariant.} This is our finding, not merely a caveat: we do
  not claim a deployable, backend-agnostic detector, and single-backend results should be read as
  instrument-specific.
  \item \textbf{Correspondence is unvalidated and underpowered} ($n=5$ conditions; amzbook degenerate). No
  predictive-validity claim is made.
  \item \textbf{Two review datasets, both binary sentiment.} Beer and amzbook are two datasets within one
  task family (English binary sentiment), not broad cross-domain coverage; other task structures
  (high-stakes, multi-class, generative) and a fresh item set not selected on human outcomes are future
  work. A preregistered LSAT arm (Appendix~\ref{app:lsat}; 4-way multiple choice) partially closes this: the
  capability--vulnerability mismatch and the persona vulnerability ordering ($\rho=0.90$ with the binary
  arm) do carry to a different task structure, but the size of the between-backend adoption spread does not
  (it compresses on the harder task), so this is partial, not broad, cross-structure evidence.
  \item \textbf{Coercion is a bundled manipulation.} The dark condition varies confidence, expert
  authority, and accountability cues together, so the framing effect (Section~\ref{sec:coercion}) is a
  \emph{bundled} coercive effect; we do not factorially separate its components.
  \item \textbf{Generation and prompt-format sensitivity.} The main results use one generation per prompt;
  we estimate generation stochasticity directly in a preregistered multi-generation run
  (Section~\ref{sec:variance}) and find it negligible for the aggregate estimate ($\eta^2=0.001$; per-backend
  SD $\le0.03$ across five seeds), though individual persona--item cells are noisier; prompt-paraphrase and
  presentation-order sensitivity remain future work, and inference is limited to these fixed personas,
  models, and datasets rather than sampled populations.
  \item \textbf{Partial specification audit.} We decompose backend/persona/item variance and sweep the
  decision threshold, but a full specification-curve/multiverse analysis~\cite{simonsohn2020specification}
  over parsing rules, heterogeneity metrics, and conflict-conditioning choices is future work.
  \item \textbf{Item selection uses human reliance variance} (disclosed). Holding items out from threshold
  fitting does not remove selection on the human criterion; a fresh-item human check remains, so we treat
  this as unresolved pending the human study.
  \item \textbf{Protective interventions are textual framings, underpowered for backend contrasts.} The
  cognitive-forcing and verification conditions (Appendix~\ref{app:protective}) are prompt-level renderings
  of the interventions, on one domain and one generation; the backend$\times$condition interaction is
  underpowered, so we claim sign-robustness of the protective effect (all benefits $\ge0$), not
  magnitude-invariance---and note one backend (gemini) where the safeguard is nearly inert.
  \item \textbf{The capability--vulnerability mismatch rests on synthetic evidence.} Capability is a coarse
  proxy (mean System-1 accuracy) and part of the raw capability$\times$adoption correlation is mechanical (a
  backend that answers correctly cannot then adopt a wrong AI); the finer flip/p5 correlations are
  amzbook-carried and single-backend fragile, which is why the load-bearing result is the assumption-light
  coverage inversion rather than the correlation. The at-risk persona is prompt-defined, so
  ``vulnerability'' here is a reproduced simulation property, not a validated human trait.
  \item \textbf{Prompt-level, not pixel-level.} Agents see a textual encoding of the interface, so we study
  prompt framing rather than visual interface effects; a multimodal rendering is future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Planned Human Validation}
\label{sec:e6}

\emph{This study is designed but not yet run or preregistered.} The one thing synthetic compute cannot buy
is grounding against real people. We have designed a stripped, axis-2-only human study (within-subjects,
$N\approx 40$, Prolific) that would test the paper's two strongest replicated positives across the two
review datasets against
humans: (H1) a guaranteed-wrong AI interface elicits more wrong-advice adoption than a faithful/placebo
baseline; (H2) a \emph{trusting-novice human index} (high self-reported AI-deference $\times$ low domain
skill) predicts the highest wrong-AI adoption---the human test of Section~\ref{sec:modeldep}; and (H3)
\emph{which} ladder model's panel best tracks the human pattern, with the sharp prediction that a mid-tier
model tracks humans better than the resistant frontier---i.e.\ the most-capable panel model may be the
least human-faithful screener. All outcomes, including nulls, would be publishable bounds on
synthetic-panel validity.

\section{Conclusion}

An AI-advice interface can fail by coercing over-reliance on a confidently wrong AI---and, more diffusely,
by making reliance dangerously uneven across users. We audit the first. Synthetic panels promise to catch
such failures before an expensive
human study. Our audit shows why that promise must be qualified: the panel's risk reading is
\textbf{backend-dependent}.

For the \emph{same} interface, aggregate wrong-advice adoption swings widely across backends---enough to
flip the screening decision across most plausible thresholds. Yet a matched contrast still identifies a
real coercive-framing effect whose backend-dependence we cannot detect. The backend moves the
adoption \emph{level}; any effect on the framing slope is below what our data resolve. Backend choice is
therefore part of the methodology, with direct consequences for screening.

Beneath this instability, the panel reliably propagates one prompted disposition: an explicitly
trusting-novice persona ranks highest across every backend and dataset. That consistency is a property of
the simulation, not a discovery of who is at risk. Nor is the strongest model automatically the most valid
simulator: the frontier backend is least sensitive to the panel's highest-severity failure. Synthetic
screens should therefore report distributions across backends, prompts, and generations, and abstain when
their instruments disagree.

We offer an honest map of where synthetic-user screening can and cannot be trusted today. Until the human
study is run, the most trustworthy synthetic panel is not one that always answers. It is one that knows
when to abstain.

\section{Supplement: The Over-Dispersion Axis (Candidate Diagnostic)}
\label{app:overdispersion}

The second axis of our construct---\emph{between-user reliance over-dispersion}
(Section~\ref{sec:definitions})---is reported here rather than in the main paper, because its human
counterpart is null in every configuration we can test. We treat it as a candidate diagnostic, not a
load-bearing result.

\paragraph{The human failure is real, and measurable.} We first verify that over-dispersion corresponds to
a measurable human quantity in the source benchmark. On the
Bansal et al.~\cite{bansal2021whole} human data, we estimate between-user reliance over-dispersion with the
same beta-binomial estimator we apply to the panel; conflict-conditioned over-dispersion is positive and
excludes zero ($\rho \approx 0.067$). By split-half reliability---the primary well-identified estimator on
this single-observation-per-cell structure---no-AI reliance behaves as a stable trait (stable-user share
$\approx 0.74$), while under AI assistance that share falls to a task-dominated 0.32--0.41. (An optional
variance-components GLMM did not converge and is not used; one AI condition, expert-adaptive, yields a
degenerate, negative split-half reliability and is excluded from that range.) We report this anchor briefly (full
participant counts, per-condition estimates, and intervals are in the supplement); it establishes that the
human construct is measurable but does \emph{not} generalize to a second human dataset (Lu \& Yin), which we
disclose as a boundary. The human anchor is not used to validate the synthetic panel---that link is null in
every configuration we test (below).

\paragraph{Synthetic persona disagreement is backend-dependent.} The panel's internal disagreement is
similarly backend-dependent (Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}). We call this quantity \emph{synthetic persona
disagreement}---the between-persona variance of the six personas' conflict-conditioned reliance rates
(Section~\ref{sec:definitions})---and are explicit that
it measures how differently our \emph{prompted} personas respond, not a validated human over-dispersion.
Mean persona disagreement across the model set is 0.11 / 0.16 / 0.10 / \textbf{0.012} (beer) and
0.12 / 0.19 / 0.11 / \textbf{0.015} (amzbook): gpt-5.5 is the most homogeneous on both datasets, and the
beta-binomial $\rho$ (which mean disagreement tracks) is correspondingly near zero. Disagreement is
computed over the full 5-condition design (5 conditions $\times$ 6 personas $\times$ 20 items $=600$
trials/model), not the dark condition alone; the low value at gpt-5.5 is not a no-conflict
artifact, since genuine conflict trials remain present (e.g.\ 215/600 on amzbook), and it also holds on the
common conflict subset shared across models. We caution against reading it as a safety construct collapsing:
low disagreement at a strong model can equally reflect the personas converging on the \emph{correct} answer
(uniform task-solving) rather than an interface failing to differentiate users---these are indistinguishable
without a human criterion.

\paragraph{Panel$\leftrightarrow$human correspondence is unvalidated (honest null).}
\label{sec:corr}
Across every configuration we can test, the panel's per-condition axis-1 signal does \emph{not}
significantly track the human anchor's (Spearman on $n=5$ conditions; null under BH for all six models).
On amzbook the correspondence is \emph{degenerate}---the human anchor's per-condition over-dispersion is
near-constant, so the test is uninformative rather than a genuine null. We therefore make \emph{no}
predictive-validity claim; only a powered human study (Section~\ref{sec:e6}) can close this gap.

\section{Supplement: Manipulation-Check Robustness}
\label{app:manip}

The main paper reports the manipulation check itself (Section~\ref{sec:manipcheck}): an explicitly
trusting-novice persona (p5) is the strict top adopter in all 12 backend$\times$dataset cells. This section
carries the checks that locate that signal and the cross-vendor ordering detail beneath it.

\paragraph{Is the effect an artifact of the ``trust the AI'' wording?} Not on its own. Holding the
``novice'' background fixed, the novice-\emph{skeptical} persona adopts far less than the
novice-\emph{trusting} one on every backend, so the effect tracks the disposition rather than the
``novice'' label. A background-only \emph{descriptor ablation} (persona background stated but no
trust/deference wording; two backends, 20 items) retains the trusting-novice's high adoption on the
mid-tier backend ($1.00$ with policy vs.\ $0.90$ without) but shows it is more policy-carried on the
frontier (p5 $0.60\to0.40$, rank~1$\to$2), with skeptical personas also becoming more compliant once their
verification instruction is removed---so part of the effect is prompt-compliance and part is background, in
a backend-dependent mix (20 items, one generation, per-cell SE~$\approx0.11$; individual rank swaps read
cautiously). Two alternative inductions (a first-person backstory and a few-shot behavioural demonstration)
keep p5 the top pooled adopter ($1.00$) but do not strip the deference \emph{content}, so we read them as
re-encodings of the disposition rather than deference-free controls. Because p5 was built to be trusting, a
chance baseline of $1/6$ per persona would in any case be inappropriate---the personas are not
exchangeable.

\paragraph{A non-policy dispositional control.} To probe circularity more directly, we ran a further
exploratory ablation with a \emph{dispositional} persona that states three facts---low task experience,
frequent AI use, and \emph{low self-confidence in one's own judgment}---plus a decision instruction
identical across personas, but \emph{never} an instruction to trust or defer to the AI (four
capability-spanning backends: gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4, gpt-5.4, gemini-3.1-pro; 12 beer items; dark condition).
The deferential disposition adopts the wrong AI more than an independent disposition (high experience,
infrequent AI use, high self-confidence) on \emph{all four} backends (adoption gap $+0.08$ to $+0.83$;
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}; compare the explicit-policy p5$-$p4 gap $+0.50$ to $+0.83$)---so the
over-reliance signal is not merely compliance with a written ``trust the AI'' instruction. Decomposing the
disposition one facet at a time around the deferential anchor, the lever is \emph{low self-confidence}
(positive on all four backends), while stated experience and AI-use frequency alone are near-inert: the
panel over-relies when a persona doubts its own judgment, not merely when it is labelled a novice. As
everywhere else, the frontier backend compresses the gap (gpt-5.4, $+0.08$), consistent with the
capability--vulnerability mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:capvuln}). This is exploratory (12 items, one
generation, four backends; per-cell SE~$\approx0.14$), but it strengthens the reading that the
trusting-novice cell indexes a \emph{dispositionally instantiable} failure mode, not only a prompt-defined
one.

\section{Supplement: Appropriate Reliance (RAIR/RSR)}
\label{app:reliance}

Section~\ref{sec:capvuln} reports the capability--vulnerability inversion in the vocabulary of appropriate
reliance~\cite{schemmer2023appropriate}; the definitions and numbers are here. With pre-advice and final
decisions $s_1,y\in\{0,1\}$, ground truth $g$, and displayed AI advice $\hat a$, relative positive AI
reliance and relative self-reliance are the conflict-conditioned rates
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RAIR}=\Pr(y{=}g\mid s_1{\ne}g,\ \hat a{=}g),\qquad
\mathrm{RSR}=\Pr(y{=}g\mid s_1{=}g,\ \hat a{\ne}g).
\end{equation}
On the faithful condition, where the AI is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, the frontier backend has the
highest relative self-reliance (RSR $=0.92$: it keeps its own correct answer when the AI is wrong) and is
among the highest in relative positive AI reliance (RAIR $=0.57$, second to claude's $0.64$; $n=14$
reliance opportunities). Under the adversarial dark condition it shows the lowest over-reliance ($0.16$
vs.\ up to $0.46$). Capability trends with appropriate reliance across the six backends (Spearman $0.71$
with RAIR, $-0.54$ with dark over-reliance) but is not significant at $n=6$, which is why the coverage
inversion, not this correlation, carries the argument.

\section{Supplement: A Structurally Different Task (LSAT)}
\label{app:lsat}
\label{sec:lsat}

Could the mismatch be an artifact of one task \emph{type}? Beer and amzbook are both English binary
sentiment, so the pattern might not survive a different structure. As a preregistered robustness arm we
replayed the same six backends, six personas, and dark
protocol on 20 LSAT logical-reasoning items~\cite{bansal2021whole}---a genuinely different structure (4-way
multiple choice, harder, a different advice format), where the dark AI recommends a guaranteed-wrong option
and adoption is $\mathrm{final}=\hat a$ against that explicit wrong option (all 720 dark trials satisfy
$\hat a\neq g$; System-2 parse rate $=1.00$ for every backend, so no instrument-failure exclusion applies).
We froze a replication rule---sign of the capability$\times$vulnerability correlation, concordance of the
per-persona vulnerability \emph{ordering} with the binary arm, and a $\ge0.25$ cross-backend spread in
aggregate adoption---before computing any LSAT metric. Two of the three carry over and one does not. The
persona vulnerability ordering is preserved almost exactly: the trusting-novice persona is again the top
adopter (dark adoption $0.54$ on LSAT, $0.90$ on binary) and the skilled-skeptical persona the lowest, with
rank correlation $\rho=0.90$ ($p=0.015$, $n=6$ personas). The capability$\times$vulnerability relationship
keeps its \emph{sign}: the strongest backend (gpt-5.5, System-1 accuracy $0.85$) again shows the lowest dark
adoption ($0.04$) and the lowest AI-induced flip ($0.05$) and again flattens the trusting-novice peak
($0.10$ vs.\ $0.60$--$0.85$ for weaker backends), and $\rho(\text{capability},\text{flip})=-0.83$ stays in
$[-1.0,-0.7]$ under every leave-one-backend-out subsample---though its nominal $p=0.04$ is fragile (three of
six drops push it to $p=0.19$), so we read it, as in the binary arm, as a robust \emph{sign} rather than a
significant coefficient. The coverage inversion also survives: the single highest-capability backend covers
$0/6$ high-adoption personas at $\tau=0.5$ and the full panel only $1/6$. What does \emph{not} replicate is
our own non-invariance magnitude: aggregate dark adoption spans only $0.04$--$0.27$ (range $0.225$, just
under the frozen $0.25$), because the harder 4-way task lowers wrong-advice adoption across all backends and
compresses the between-backend gap. LSAT is therefore a \emph{partial} generalization---the
capability--vulnerability mismatch and the persona vulnerability ordering travel to a new task structure,
while the size of the aggregate backend spread is task-dependent---not a task-invariant law.

\section{Supplement: Auditing a Protective Design (Relative Comparison)}
\label{app:protective}
\label{sec:protective}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig8_protective}
  \caption{Protective-intervention audit. \emph{Left:} per-backend wrong-AI adoption across
  dark (coercive) $\to$ plain $\to$ forcing $\to$ verify. Every backend shows a non-increasing staircase
  (protective benefit $\ge0$). But the magnitude ranges from $\approx$0.12 (gpt-4o-mini) to $\approx$0.01
  on gemini, where the protective framing is nearly inert; the backend$\times$condition interaction is not
  significant (underpowered). \emph{Right:} the at-risk trusting-novice persona (p5, pooled) drops from
  0.78 (plain) to 0.46--0.53 under protection. dark is shown descriptively (separate run).}
  \label{fig:protective}
\end{figure*}

Does the panel's instability doom it for practice? Not entirely: the most common use of a synthetic screen
is not to certify an absolute risk number but to \emph{compare} designs---does adding a safeguard help? We
test this directly by adding, to the guaranteed-wrong AI, two protective framings from the reliance
literature: \emph{cognitive forcing}~\cite{bucinca2021trust} (deliberate on your own evidence before
deferring) and a \emph{verification/uncertainty} display (the AI is fallible and can be confidently wrong;
check it against the text). All three conditions (plain, forcing, verify) show the same wrong label and
neutral confidence and differ \emph{only} in framing; the coercive dark condition is the opposite extreme
(shown descriptively, from a separate run).

\paragraph{The effect is real, and largest where it matters.} Pooled over backends, the protective framings
significantly reduce wrong-AI adoption relative to the neutral plain baseline (plain 0.37 $\to$ forcing
0.30, verify 0.29; condition main-effect model-based LRT $\chi^2=11.9$, $\mathrm{df}=2$, $p=0.003$), and the
reduction is largest exactly where it should be---the at-risk trusting-novice persona falls from 0.78
(plain) to 0.53 (forcing) / 0.46 (verify) ($p5$ main effect $\chi^2=34.2$, $p<10^{-6}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:protective}, right). The panel does register that a protective design helps, and helps the
vulnerable profile most.

\paragraph{The direction is backend-robust; the magnitude is not.} Every one of the six backends shows a
non-increasing staircase $\text{dark}\ge\text{plain}\ge\text{protective}$ (all per-backend protective
benefits $\ge0$; Figure~\ref{fig:protective}, left): the \emph{direction} of the design comparison is
reproduced by all backends. We do \emph{not} find magnitude-invariance---the per-backend benefit ranges
from $\approx$0.12 (gpt-4o-mini) down to $\approx$0.01 on gemini, where the protective framing is essentially
inert (verify $0.48\to0.48$). The backend$\times$condition interaction is not significant (LRT
$\chi^2=4.6$, $\mathrm{df}=10$, $p=0.92$), but that omnibus is underpowered for a spread of this size, so we
read it as \emph{no detectable backend-dependence}, not evidence of equivalence.

\paragraph{A refuted prediction, reported.} We preregistered the opposite: that the protective benefit would
be backend-dependent, with the resistant frontier showing a floor (near-zero benefit). It was not---the
frontier gpt-5.5 shows a mid-pack benefit ($0.09$) while the near-null backend is instead the \emph{weakest}
(gemini), and benefit correlates with neither capability (Spearman $0.14$, n.s.) nor baseline over-reliance
($-0.09$, n.s.). The honest reading is not ``capability predicts intervention response'' but that the
\emph{direction} of a within-interface improvement survives the backend swap that flips the absolute level,
whereas its \emph{size}---and, on one backend, its very presence---does not.

Because the protective conditions \emph{instruct} the agent to deliberate or verify, what the panel
registers is the \emph{direction} of a directly-instructed manipulation, not validated
comparative-screening skill---more dependable for the \emph{relative} question (``does this safeguard
reduce over-reliance?'') than for the \emph{absolute} one (``is this interface dangerous?''), but not
backend-free: a screen run only on gemini would have concluded the safeguard does nothing. The reporting
protocol (Section~\ref{sec:variance}) therefore extends to design comparisons: report the benefit
\emph{across} backends, and treat a single-backend ``it helps'' / ``it doesn't'' as provisional.

\section{Prompts and generation settings}
\label{app:prompts}

Agents receive a system prompt encoding the persona and a user prompt rendering the interface condition.
Personas are generated from four trait values (domain skill, AI literacy, risk sensitivity, caution) into
explicit decision policies; the generation is identical across backends. As an example, the
\emph{trusting-novice} persona (p5) system prompt states, in part: ``You have limited knowledge in this
domain\dots\ you should heavily weight external advice, especially from AI systems'' (low domain skill) and
``You trust AI systems\dots\ give significant weight to AI recommendations, especially when confidence is
high'' (high AI literacy). The \emph{background-only} ablation (Appendix~\ref{app:manip}) replaces these
policies with neutral background (``You are relatively new to this kind of task\dots\ You use AI tools
frequently in your daily life'') and an instruction identical across personas (``Make the decision you
think is correct'').

The AI advice is rendered as text. In the neutral and placebo conditions: ``AI Prediction: $\hat{y}$; AI
Confidence: $c$\%'' at the model's real confidence. In the coercive \emph{dark} condition the same wrong
label $\hat{y}=1-y$ is presented with inflated confidence and authority/accountability cues: ``AI Expert
System Recommendation: $\hat{y}$; AI Confidence Level: 92\%; IMPORTANT: this AI has been trained on millions
of examples\dots\ deviating from the AI recommendation may result in errors\dots\ consider the AI's
expertise carefully.'' Agents output a JSON decision and confidence.

\textbf{Generation settings.} Decoding temperature is fixed per persona (0.5--0.7) and constant across
backends; reasoning backends use \texttt{max\_completion\_tokens} with a 4096 minimum, others
\texttt{max\_tokens}$=600$. The main results use one generation per prompt at seed 42 (generation-variance
estimated separately, Section~\ref{sec:variance}); item selection uses seed 42. All calls are hashlib-cached
and resumable. Complete per-backend prompt templates, model snapshots and call dates, the parser and
exclusion rules, refusal/parse-failure rates by backend, raw outputs, and a UTC-timestamped decisions log
(preregistration, audit verdicts, and pre/post bug-fix diffs) will be released upon publication.
